Prithvi-Grid Innovation Playbook, Volume 2: 24 Compost-Craft and Operations Features
Prepared for the Prithvi-Grid founding team · 24 October 2026 · Volume 2 of the feature suggestions, to be converted into agent build prompts one feature or cluster at a time
Purpose and scope of this volume
Volume 1 of this playbook proposed twenty-four features concentrated on the Smart Spear itself, the evidence that comes out of it and the commercial growth of the business: the wake scheduler, the evidence chain, pit personality profiles, Aaj ka Plan, WhatsApp voice logging, the decision inbox, the group control tower and so on. This second volume deliberately does not repeat any of them, and it does not repeat the features already described in the enterprise upgrade master prompt (Kaam Ka Saboot v2, AI Sanjivani, the odor predictor, the overload engine, batch management, the compliance matrix, inspection mode and so on). Where a new feature builds on an earlier one, it says so explicitly and refers to it as Volume 1 Feature N or as a master-prompt workstream, so your agent can see exactly what to reuse.
The focus of this volume is the craft and management of turning organic wet waste into compost and manure. The first half looks at the people who actually do the work and the manager who must run it: how a heap is loaded, turned, watered, protected from pests, cured, screened, tested and turned into a product that can be sold. These are the parts of the process where a good tool changes daily results, and where today’s system mostly watches rather than helps. The second half looks at the pages and workflows a manager uses to see, maintain, monitor and manage the whole operation: a digital site map, an equipment register, standard operating procedures, a daily close-out, correction requests, in-context conversations, a time machine for pits and for configuration, a yard wallboard, client-side node care and a self-serve report studio. Those are the supporting screens that make an enterprise client feel that the platform is a complete operating system for their compost yard, not only a sensor dashboard.
How the features were chosen
I applied five tests. The feature must be genuinely useful to the person who does or supervises compost work, which means I imagined a sweeper at five in the morning, a supervisor at the pit with a phone, a facility manager in the evening, and an HQ agronomist reviewing a fleet. It must use what your system uniquely has, namely live pit sensors, a verified labour ledger, and a growing record of what actually happened after each intervention. It must be buildable by an agentic AI on your existing foundations, so each feature lists what it reuses. It must be honest, so estimates are labelled, observations are treated as observations and legal or agronomic claims are marked for expert review. And it must be safe to ship in stages, so each feature can start as a simple, flagged version and mature as data accumulates.
The same ground rules as the master prompt apply to every feature here: deterministic code makes decisions and Gemini is the only AI provider, used for explanation, language, perception and drafting through the AI Gateway; every sensitive action is audited; tenant isolation is enforced centrally; everything works offline for the ground-level user; every user-visible string is internationalised with Hindi, Hinglish and English; anything involving law, regulation, pesticides, laboratory standards, wages or product claims carries a LEGAL-REVIEW or EXPERT-REVIEW tag; and Hindi wording is marked NEEDS-NATIVE-REVIEW until a fluent speaker approves it.
How each feature is written
Every feature uses the same headings so you can paste any one of them into a build prompt. Why it matters describes the real situation in the yard. Who uses it and when names the roles. How it works gives the logic in enough detail for an agent to design it. Data model lists the records and fields to add. Screens and experience describes what the worker, the manager and HQ see. AI role states exactly what Gemini does, and often that it does nothing. Integration says what existing parts it connects to. Build notes and guardrails lists the traps. Measure and honest limits lists the numbers to watch and what the feature cannot do. Priority carries a label of Must-win, Strong or Opportunistic and a rough effort estimate in engineer-weeks for a human-reviewed agent build; treat these as order-of-magnitude guidance.
The features are grouped in four parts. Part A, features 1 to 8, covers the craft at the pit: turning, observing, loading, holding waste, water, leachate, pests and worker strain. Part B, features 9 to 13, follows the compost from pit to product: curing and screening, mass balance, quality testing, value-added manure and selling. Part C, features 14 to 19, covers management pages and workflows: site map, equipment, procedures, day close, corrections and conversations. Part D, features 20 to 24, covers the viewing, monitoring and maintenance pages: the pit time machine, the yard wallboard, node care rounds, the report studio and the configuration time machine. The document ends with a recommended build order and the questions I need answered before I write the build prompts.
Part A: The craft at the pit
Feature 1: Palatna Coach, Live Guided Turning
Why it matters. Turning is the most frequent and the most inconsistently performed task in composting. Too little turning leaves anaerobic pockets in the core where methane builds and odor begins. Too much turning cools the pile, wastes labour and fuel, and slows the thermophilic phase. Workers turn by habit, so duration, depth and pattern vary enormously between people and shifts. Kaam Ka Saboot can tell afterwards whether the sensors saw a turning signature, but nobody coaches the worker during the task. A coach that stays beside the worker, saying in simple Hindi what to do next and when to stop, raises quality immediately, shortens training of new staff and reduces wasted effort.
Who uses it and when. The Safai Mitra uses it every time a turning task starts, usually from an Aaj ka Plan card. The supervisor configures turning profiles for the facility. The manager reads a coaching-oriented quality page. HQ agronomists own the master profiles and approve changes.
How it works. A Turning Profile is a structured record defined per pit profile, pit size and method (hand fork, bucket loader, mechanical turner). It describes an ordered set of segments such as Prepare, Open the surface, Turn the outer layer into the core, Turn the core outward, Check, Finish and Close. Each segment has an expected duration range scaled to the pit size, a spoken instruction, a picture, a safety note, and optional sensor criteria. When the worker taps Palatna shuru, the coach checks the hazard banner and the PPE checklist, then asks the command queue for a forced reading and, where a wake appointment exists (Volume 1 Feature 1), uses the awake node to collect a baseline. The coach then walks the worker through each segment with a large timer ring, a single instruction and audio. If the node is awake and charged, the coach also collects mid-task readings and interprets them against the expected signature: a release of trapped methane followed by a decline suggests the core has been opened properly; a short temperature dip followed by a rebound suggests oxygen has reached active material; a drop that is too deep or too long suggests over-turning and the coach advises stopping and covering. If methane is still high at the end of a segment, the coach suggests one more pass on the lower layer. When sensors are asleep or untrusted, the coach falls back to timers and checklist steps without pretending to know more than it does.
The worker can end the session at any time. If they stop early, an icon picker records the reason (tired, other urgent work, machine problem, safety concern) and the session is saved as partial rather than discarded. The coach computes a turning quality label of Thorough, Light or Excessive from segment completion, duration and any sensor evidence, and shows it kindly: it never uses accusing language. After two to six hours the system looks at what happened next (did temperature recover, did methane fall, did odor risk drop) and stores the outcome beside the session, so the platform learns which techniques work for which pit types. The session itself is strong supporting evidence for Kaam Ka Saboot because it has timestamps, segment taps and sensor snapshots, but it never decides a verdict alone. Proficient workers can switch to Expert mode with fewer prompts, and coaching variants can be tested across willing facilities, with an agronomist approving every variant and no experiments allowed where safety is involved.
Data model. TurningProfile with id, pit profile, size range, method, version, segments (name, duration range, instruction keys, picture, safety note, sensor criteria), and an approval record. TurningSession with pit, worker, method, start and end times, per-segment timestamps, sensor snapshots with quality flags, end reason, quality label, source (coach or manual), the linked Labour_Execution_Ledger entry and the later outcome measurements. CoachingEvent entries for each prompt shown and each tap, kept for audit but retained only for a limited period.
Screens and experience. The worker sees a giant green Palatna shuru button, a PPE tick screen, then one segment per screen with a picture, a timer ring and a voice prompt, haptic buzz at each change, a screen that stays awake, and a final confirmation with the Verified, Checking or Check again status. The manager sees a Turning Quality page with session counts, average duration against the profile, the distribution of quality labels and the outcome after turning. The page is framed for coaching, shows aggregated trends first, and lets the manager open a session to view its timeline. HQ sees a profile editor with a preview, an approval workflow and a comparison of outcomes across profiles.
AI role. Gemini phrases coaching prompts in friendly Hindi or Hinglish from approved templates and maps a small set of spoken commands (agla, ruk, dobara) to a closed command list with confidence scores, so a worker with wet hands can control the coach by voice. Gemini never decides when to stop; rules do.
Integration. Aaj ka Plan launches it. Kaam Ka Saboot v2 and the evidence strength score consume it. The wake scheduler and command queue provide the sensor appointments. The safety checklist and hazard banner from Workstream O appear at the start. Sanjivani can add recipe steps (for example adding bulking material during the turn) as an extra segment.
Build notes and guardrails. The whole coach must work offline, cache its audio, run smoothly on a two-gigabyte phone, and never block a worker from finishing. Profiles are data, not code, versioned and expert-reviewed. The coach must never encourage anyone to enter or lean into a pit, and must show confined-space safety reminders at the top. Quality labels are information for supervision, not for automatic penalties.
Measure and honest limits. Track the share of turnings done with the coach, quality distribution, time per turning, Inconclusive verdict rate, odor reports within a day of turning and outcomes after turning. The coach cannot see inside the whole pile, since one probe measures one location, so its sensor feedback is advisory and the interface says so. If workers find it patronising, the skip and Expert mode controls matter, and adoption data will show it.
Priority. Must-win. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Feature 2: Sense Check, Field-Truth Capture and Hand-Test Calibration
Why it matters. Sensors drift, laboratories are slow and expensive, and yet every experienced worker already performs quick checks by hand and eye: squeezing a fistful to judge moisture, smelling the heap, feeling the warmth of a stick pushed into the core, noticing colour, texture, fungus, flies or pooling liquid. These human observations are valuable ground truth, and today the system ignores them. Capturing them in ten seconds with icons gives you a labelled dataset that can calibrate sensors, validate engines, expose drift early and reduce disputes, and it treats workers as expert observers rather than mere task executors.
Who uses it and when. Workers do a quick Sense Check on a schedule and whenever prompted. Supervisors do a deeper weekly check. Technicians use the results during calibration. HQ data staff use the labelled dataset for modelling.
How it works. An Observation Kit is a versioned set of icon-based observations designed with an agronomist. The squeeze test has four outcomes shown as pictures: water drips out, only a few drops, holds its shape and crumbles when poked, or falls apart dry. The smell scale has five levels: earthy, mildly sour, strongly sour like vinegar, sharp like ammonia, and rotten-egg or sulphur. The stick-heat test has four outcomes from cold to too hot to hold, always using a stick and never bare hands deep in the pile. Colour has dark, brown and fresh-green options, texture runs from recognisable scraps to crumbly soil-like, and a visible-signs row covers white fungus, flies, maggots, ants, pooled liquid and steam. Each observation records pit, depth (surface, middle, core), time and an optional photo.
Prompts are targeted rather than random. The system asks for a squeeze test when the moisture reading is near a threshold, when its sensor trust has dropped, or when two sensors disagree, because that is when a human observation adds the most information (value-of-information prompting). It also asks after interventions and on a light schedule. The captured observations serve five purposes. First, calibration: each observation is paired with the nearest trustworthy sensor readings and an ordinal mapping is fitted per pit, so the system can see that this pit’s moisture sensor consistently reads higher than the hand test suggests and propose recalibration or flag drift. Second, engine validation: smell scores become labels for the odor predictor, and squeeze categories validate the moisture logic. Third, corroboration: a recent consistent human observation raises the evidence strength of a claim. Fourth, kind coaching: if a worker’s observation differs sharply from peers and sensors, the app offers a short picture lesson rather than a correction. Fifth, observation-to-action: a sharp ammonia smell can propose an action card such as adding bulking material, routed through Sanjivani or Aaj ka Plan. Observer reliability is estimated internally from agreement with sensors and peers, used only to weight labels, and never shown as a ranking.
Data model. ObservationKit definitions with versions. Observation records with pit, observer, type, value, depth, timestamp, photo reference, a snapshot of the nearest sensor readings and their quality flags, and a label confidence. CalibrationPair records linking an observation and a sensor reading for a given sensor. DriftFlag records produced by the pairing analysis, each with status and the human who reviewed it.
Screens and experience. The worker sees an icon grid titled Pit ka haal, with large pictures and three to five taps in total, audio for each icon, a thank-you and, when relevant, a one-tap suggested action. The manager sees observation icons plotted on the sensor charts, a calibration suggestions page (for example, moisture sensor in pit 2 reads higher than hand tests in six of the last eight checks, review calibration?) with accept or dismiss, and a short list of pits where human and sensor views disagree. HQ gets a dataset explorer with agreement statistics and a clean export for modelling.
AI role. Gemini can classify an optional photo into the same texture and colour categories as a secondary opinion, always displayed beside the worker’s own choice, and can translate free-text notes. It never changes a calibration or label by itself.
Integration. Calibration management (Workstream A8), Sensor Trust Score (Volume 1 Feature 4), Evidence Strength (Volume 1 Feature 6), Pit Personality (Volume 1 Feature 3), the odor feedback loop (B3) and Sanjivani.
Build notes and guardrails. Have an agronomist approve the kit, and mark icons and Hindi wording NEEDS-NATIVE-REVIEW. Keep prompts few and valuable to avoid survey fatigue. Proposals for recalibration go to a technician or manager and are never applied automatically. Record that squeeze categories are coarse and ordinal, so they can detect drift and gross errors but cannot recover an exact moisture percentage.
Measure and honest limits. Observations per pit per week, agreement between observation and sensor categories, drift detected earlier than before, technician visits avoided and calibration frequency. Human observations are subjective, which is why several observers and ordinal categories are used, and the interface presents them as observations rather than measurements.
Priority. Must-win, because it starts a data flywheel. Four to five engineer-weeks.
Feature 3: Parat Planner and Live Mix Meter
Why it matters. Loading a pit is where most of the outcome is decided. Kitchen wet waste is high in nitrogen and moisture and low in structure, so it must be layered with carbon-rich, dry, bulky material to reach a workable carbon-to-nitrogen balance, a moisture level the microbes like and enough air space for oxygen to move. Workers usually load by guess. Too much wet waste produces sludge, leachate, flies and an anaerobic start; too much dry material makes the heap slow to heat. Managers cannot see the quality of a mix, and Sanjivani can only rescue a pit after it has gone wrong. Bulking inventory (Volume 1 Feature 12) tracks stock and the blending calculator in the master prompt is a desk tool, but nothing yet guides the physical act of loading while it is happening.
Who uses it and when. The Safai Mitra uses it every time a pit is loaded. The supervisor defines house recipes for the facility. The manager reviews mix quality per load. HQ agronomists maintain the material library.
How it works. The Material Library is a versioned, expert-reviewed list of the materials a facility handles: cooked food waste, raw vegetable peels, fruit waste, tea and coffee grounds, banquet leftovers, garden leaves, sawdust, dry leaves, cocopeat, straw, shredded cardboard, and any local additions. Each has typical ranges (not single values) for carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, moisture, a structure or porosity rating, notes and limits (for example oily food, citrus and onion proportions, salty waste), and a bulk density per container actually used at the facility, such as a bucket or trolley. A facility can override values locally and the system learns overrides from laboratory results (Feature 11).
House Recipes express the desired blend in worker language, such as 2 balti geela to 1 balti sukha, with an acceptable range, defined per waste type and pit profile. When a loading task starts, the planner reads the expected load (from the intake diary or the banquet planner) and produces a layer plan: a base of coarse dry material for drainage, alternating wet and dry layers with suggested thickness, and a cover layer. It also produces a staging list of what to place beside the pit before starting and checks stock. As the worker taps a material icon and a count for each container, the Live Mix Meter updates three simple gauges (carbon-to-nitrogen band, moisture band, air space) in green, amber or red, accompanied by a single next-step suggestion such as ab 1 balti sukha daalein. The gauges use ranges and display uncertainty honestly, for instance by showing a band on the gauge rather than a needle. The meter warns about layer thickness, large oily loads and the share of troublesome materials, and a sudden-large-load mode advises splitting the load across pits using the overload engine.
After loading, the planner closes the session with a Load Record, which becomes the input record for the batch (Workstream L1). Over the next one to three days it compares the predicted and actual heating response and refines the facility’s material parameters, so a facility whose sawdust is consistently damper than the library value gets a better estimate. A Pre-mix Station option supports facilities with space, where wet and dry material are blended in a bay before going to the pit.
Data model. Material and MaterialLocalOverride, HouseRecipe, LayerPlan, ContainerCalibration (kilograms per bucket or trolley for each material), LoadSession with pit, batch, entries (material, container count, estimated mass, time), the meter timeline, deviation from plan and the later heating outcome, and MixMeterConfig for thresholds.
Screens and experience. The worker chooses the pit and sees a staging list with large pictures, then a loading screen with three meters, big material icons with plus and minus counters, and a clear Theek hai confirmation. A one-time wizard called Balti ka wazan weighs a bucket of each material to calibrate it. The manager sees a Mix Quality page listing loads with their final meter state, deviation from plan and outcome after heating, and can print a staging sheet. HQ sees the library editor, local overrides and a comparison of facilities.
AI role. Gemini can identify materials in a photo of a waste cart and propose entries for confirmation (reusing the vision approach of Workstream K4), and can phrase advice in Hindi. All estimation is deterministic code.
Integration. Bulking inventory decrements stock automatically. The banquet planner supplies the expected load. The overload engine checks capacity. Batch management receives the load record. Sanjivani uses the same material library for recovery recipes, and Pit Personality learns from the heating outcome.
Build notes and guardrails. Treat every material value as a range, never as a precise number, and require expert review. Make it work offline and keep the interface to three meters. Deviations from the recommended mix are recorded without blame, and the worker can always override. The container calibration step is essential, so the interface must make it quick and remind the facility to redo it when containers change.
Measure and honest limits. Share of loads that finish in the green band, time to reach the target temperature after loading, leachate and fly incidents, and Sanjivani alerts in the first week after loading. Carbon-to-nitrogen values vary widely in real waste, so the meter is guidance based on estimates, not a guarantee, and the interface says so.
Priority. Must-win. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Feature 4: Waste Age Clock and Holding Area Control
Why it matters. Wet waste begins to ferment and attract flies within hours. If it waits a day in a kitchen corridor, a ward collection room or a holding bin before it reaches the pit, it arrives already acidic and smelly and gives the compost a poor start. Most facilities do not know how long their waste waits, because collection rounds are informal and depend on who is available, and nobody measures freshness at the moment of loading. This is a hidden cause of odor complaints, fly problems and acid crashes that the pit sensors only detect later.
Who uses it and when. Kitchen, banquet or housekeeping staff mark containers as filled. The Safai Mitra collects and loads. The manager controls collection routines and reviews freshness. HQ sets default age limits.
How it works. Define waste streams and holding points: kitchen bins, banquet bins, ward or floor collection rooms, a central holding area and pit-side staging. Every container has a QR label. Three events are recorded by scanning or tapping: filled or sealed, collected and loaded into a pit. A fourth event, discarded elsewhere, exists for honest accounting. The system computes the Waste Age of each container as the loading time minus the filling time, or, where only collection is logged, a stream-level estimate with its uncertainty shown. Age limits are configured per stream and temperature band by an expert, because cooked food in an Indore summer deserves a shorter limit than garden waste in winter; the agent must not hard-code any value. The Age Clock screen shows every open container with a coloured ring that moves from green through amber to red, and the system warns before a container reaches its limit, for example Banquet bin 3 ko 4 ghante ho gaye.
When limits are approaching, the system proposes actions: add a collection round to Aaj ka Plan, cover the bin, move it to a cooler place, or sprinkle an approved dry material to reduce odor and flies (expert-approved). A Collection Round Planner uses the Digital Site Map (Feature 14) to order pickups sensibly and suggests round times that match kitchen service patterns, such as after breakfast and after lunch. Over time the system measures how age relates to downstream results, such as pH at loading and the early heating rate, and passes that to Pit Personality and the odor models. It also detects orphans: containers marked filled that are never collected or loaded within a set time, which may mean waste is being dumped elsewhere, and raises an alert that links to mass balance (Feature 10). A scope guard keeps non-compostable and biomedical streams out of the tracking, and a visible banner reminds hospital users that only designated organic streams belong here.
Data model. HoldingPoint, Container (QR, type, capacity, stream), WasteEvent (container, event type, time, worker, optional mass), WasteAgeSnapshot per container, and AgeLimitPolicy by stream and temperature band, with versions and approval.
Screens and experience. The worker sees Kachra Ghadi, a list of containers sorted by age with large rings and two big buttons, Uthaya and Daala, plus a scan control. Kitchen staff use a minimal no-login link reached by scanning the container QR and tapping Bin bhar gaya. The manager sees a freshness dashboard with an hour-by-hour strip, the share of containers loaded within their limit, the worst sources and trends. HQ sees the policy editor and fleet-wide freshness comparisons.
AI role. None is required. Gemini may phrase reminders, and an optional photo of a bin can be sent for a fill-level estimate that a person confirms.
Integration. The Digital Site Map and Aaj ka Plan, the banquet planner, the intake ledger (Workstream M1), the odor complaint loop, Pit Personality and mass balance.
Build notes and guardrails. Print container QR labels during onboarding and support offline scanning. Age limits are expert-defined and marked EXPERT-REVIEW, particularly for hospital hygiene guidance. Frame the data as process information and not as blame for the kitchen team. If filling times are not recorded, degrade to collection-to-load age and say so.
Measure and honest limits. Median waste age, share loaded within limit, fly complaints and early acid events against age. The feature depends on people tapping a button, so its quality depends on how simple the kitchen interaction is and on manager follow-up.
Priority. Strong. Four to five engineer-weeks.
Feature 5: Water Budget and Dosing Assistant
Why it matters. After temperature and aeration, moisture is the most important lever in composting. Workers water by feel with hoses of unknown flow, so overwatering causes leachate and an anaerobic pit, and underwatering stalls the process. Water also costs money, is scarce in Indore’s summers and is a sustainability metric that hotels and hospitals increasingly track. Facilities rarely know how much water their composting uses, and instructions such as pani chhidkein are too vague to follow consistently.
Who uses it and when. The Safai Mitra waters pits, usually after an Aaj ka Plan prompt. The manager sets budgets and reviews use. HQ agronomists approve dosing rules.
How it works. The Dosing Assistant starts from the current moisture estimate (weighted by sensor trust and any recent Sense Check), the target band for the pit profile, the estimated pit mass from Load Records and the overload engine, and the pit’s learned moisture response (Volume 1 Feature 3), which says how much moisture a litre of water adds for this pit. It computes a recommended water volume as a range with uncertainty and expresses it in the worker’s own units: buckets, cans, or hose-minutes. A Hose Test wizard calibrates each hose or can by timing how long a bucket takes to fill.
The assistant does not recommend one big dose. It proposes staged dosing: add about half, wait a set time, then re-check with a forced sensor reading or a squeeze test, and decide whether to continue, which prevents overshooting. It adjusts for the weather forecast (skip if rain is due, add earlier if a heatwave is coming) and for time of day, and follows expert-approved rules about avoiding watering that would cool an active pile too much. After each watering the system compares the predicted and actual moisture change and updates the pit’s response estimate with shrinkage toward the profile value.
The Water Budget gives each facility and pit a monthly allowance based on history and forecast intake, tracks actual use from logs (and, if hardware is later added, from a flow meter on the hose line, recorded as a hardware proposal), and tags the source of each litre: municipal supply, borewell, recycled water, rainwater or recirculated leachate (Feature 6). Recycled water carries quality rules (chlorine, salinity, contaminants) that an expert must approve before use, tagged EXPERT-REVIEW and LEGAL-REVIEW where regulations apply. Water Use Intensity, litres per hundred kilograms of waste processed, is compared with the facility’s own history, and anomalies such as sudden high use (a leak or over-watering) or unexpectedly low use raise a flag. The assistant also uses the watering event as an expected-signature input to Kaam Ka Saboot: a moisture response that does not appear after a claimed watering may mean channelling, evaporation or a failing sensor, and the system says so rather than assuming fraud.
Data model. WaterEvent (pit, estimated litres, method, source, time, worker, conversion used), WaterSource with quality rules, ContainerFlowCalibration (litres per minute or per container), DosePlan (pit, current estimate, target, recommended range, steps, assumptions) and WaterBudget (period, allowance, actual).
Screens and experience. The worker sees a Paani ki matra card showing the range, for example 2 se 3 balti, a Step 1 button, a wait timer, a prompt for a squeeze test or sensor re-check and an end button. The manager sees a Water page with use against budget, intensity by pit and source, and anomalies. HQ sees response coefficients and fleet comparisons.
AI role. Phrasing of prompts only.
Integration. Pit Personality, Sense Check, Weather Playbooks, the Parat Planner, the Leachate Loop, the Day Close checklist and Kaam Ka Saboot v2.
Build notes and guardrails. Always show ranges and use re-check steps. Require container calibration before volumes are trusted, and label volumes as estimates until it is done. Use conservative defaults, record assumptions, and require expert approval for recycled-water use.
Measure and honest limits. Time in the moisture band, water per hundred kilograms of waste, leachate incidents and overshoot events. Mass and moisture estimates carry uncertainty, so recommendations are advice that the worker confirms by the re-check step.
Priority. Strong. Four to five engineer-weeks.
Feature 6: Leachate Loop Manager
Why it matters. Leachate, the dark liquid that drains from wet waste, is simultaneously a problem and a resource. Left alone it pools, smells, breeds flies, makes the ground slippery and can contaminate drains and soil. Handled properly it carries nutrients that can be recirculated to keep an active pile moist or, after suitable treatment, used as a liquid fertiliser. Most facilities ignore it until a smell or complaint appears, nobody measures how much is produced, and nobody can show an auditor where it went. The problem is also a diagnostic signal: more leachate than expected means the pile is too wet or the mix is wrong, and none when it is expected can mean a blocked drain.
Who uses it and when. The Safai Mitra performs leachate rounds, recirculation and drain cleaning. The manager monitors volumes and handling routes. HQ agronomists define recirculation rules.
How it works. Model each leachate point: pit-base drains, collection sumps, tanks and drain channels. Each has a QR label. Level is recorded by a quick icon (empty, quarter, half, three-quarters, full) or by a dipstick reading in centimetres converted to litres using the tank’s recorded dimensions. A small float-switch or ultrasonic level sensor on the tank is recorded as a hardware proposal, with the software ready to accept its readings. Events record what happened to the liquid: collected, recirculated (to which pit and how many litres), diluted and used as liquid manure (dilution ratio and place of use), sent to an approved disposal route, or lost to overflow. Drain inspections record cleaning and blockages.
The system builds an expectation of leachate for each pit from the wet fraction of its load, recent rainfall and the pit’s history. If actual volume is far above expectation, it flags overwetting or a poor mix and links to the Parat Planner outcome; if it is far below, it flags a possible blocked drain. A rising level that outpaces the forecast, combined with a rain forecast, triggers a pre-emptive emptying task through the Weather Playbooks. Recirculation follows rules defined by an expert and tagged EXPERT-REVIEW: it is allowed only onto pits in the active hot phase and above a configured temperature, never onto curing or finished compost, within a volume limit, and subject to stricter rules in hospital settings. Recirculated litres count towards the water budget (Feature 5). An optional Leachate Tea workflow records batches, aeration time, the use-by period and where it was applied, and carries warnings about use on edible crops that must be validated by an expert before the workflow is enabled for any client. A safe-handling checklist reminds the worker about gloves, never mixing with drinking water and hand-washing.
Over time the system reports leachate litres per hundred kilograms of waste by pit, stream and season, and compares them with the pit state, so managers can see which loading habits create wet pits. All liquid movements enter the mass balance ledger (Feature 10).
Data model. LeachatePoint with type, capacity and dimensions; LeachateLevelReading with source (manual or sensor); LeachateEvent with type, litres, source and destination; RecirculationRule with conditions and approvals; TeaBatch; and DrainInspection with photos and findings.
Screens and experience. The worker follows a Paani-Rasa round, a list of points with level icons, and uses large buttons for Collected, Recirculated and Cleaned, with a photo option. The manager sees a leachate page with current levels, an overflow forecast, volumes by pit and a tea batch register. HQ sees the rules editor and fleet comparisons.
AI role. Gemini can classify a photo of a blocked drain or pooling liquid as a suggestion, and can phrase reminders. Rules and volumes are computed by code.
Integration. Water Budget, Mass Balance, Weather Playbooks, the Digital Site Map for point locations, the Pest workflow (standing liquid breeds flies), the Equipment register for pumps, and the compliance calendar for any discharge consent.
Build notes and guardrails. Anything about discharge to drains, use as fertiliser or disposal is a LEGAL-REVIEW and EXPERT-REVIEW item; the system records what happened and never tells a client that a practice is lawful. Make level capture quick and offline-capable, and treat manual levels as estimates with a visible uncertainty.
Measure and honest limits. Leachate litres per hundred kilograms, overflow events, blockages found early, and tea batches produced. Manual level estimates are rough, and the system cannot analyse the chemistry of the liquid, so it records handling rather than certifying quality.
Priority. Strong. Four to five engineer-weeks.
Feature 7: Pest and Vermin Control Workflow
Why it matters. Compost yards attract flies, maggots, cockroaches, rodents, birds, ants and stray animals. For a hotel this means complaints and reputational risk, and for a hospital it is an infection-control concern that audit committees examine closely. Today responses are reactive and often chemical-heavy. The master prompt includes vision detection and the odor complaint loop logs complaints, but there is no structured, evidence-keeping pest management process that prevents problems, tracks pressure, directs approved treatments and produces the records that hygiene audits ask for.
Who uses it and when. Workers do inspections and simple preventive actions. Supervisors schedule and review. The manager sees the pest map and vendor records. An external pest-control vendor gets a limited role to record visits. HQ defines policies.
How it works. The workflow follows an integrated pest management structure. First, monitoring: scheduled inspections per pit, holding area and perimeter, using large icons for flies (none, few, many, swarm), maggots, rodent signs (droppings, burrows, gnawing), cockroaches, birds, stray animals and ants, together with sticky-trap counts per trap per week with a photo. Second, a Pest Pressure Index for each zone combining inspections, trap counts, complaints, warm and humid weather, waste age (Feature 4), cover status and leachate presence (Feature 6), shown as a simple level. Third, prevention actions mapped to causes: cover the pit promptly with a soil or dry-material layer, fix drainage, shorten waste age, close bins, repair fencing and gates. Fourth, action thresholds set by an expert, above which the system offers treatment options from an approved list, ordered from least to most intrusive: physical barriers, cover layers, traps, approved organic or biological methods, and finally licensed chemical treatment by a vendor. For anything chemical the system records product, dose, applicator and licence reference, re-entry interval and location, and marks the record LEGAL-REVIEW; the AI never suggests chemical products or doses. Fifth, vendor visits are scheduled, the vendor completes a checklist and uploads a report, and the visit appears in the compliance calendar. Sixth, effectiveness is measured by comparing the pressure index before and after each action, so the system learns what works at which site and season. Hospital zones near wards use stricter thresholds and send a swarm or rodent finding to the incident path.
Photos help but never decide. A photo of a trap or a pit surface can be sent for a suggested species category and rough count, which the worker confirms or corrects. A rodent-proofing checklist covers gaps, lids, drainage and food sources. The evidence pack bundles inspections, trap counts, treatments and effectiveness for an audit.
Data model. PestInspection with zone, observations and photos; TrapReading; PestPressureIndex snapshots; PestAction or TreatmentRecord with product, dose, applicator, licence reference and re-entry interval; VendorVisit; and PestPolicy with thresholds, approved methods and zone rules.
Screens and experience. The worker taps through a short Keeda-Makoda check with big icons and takes a weekly trap photo. The manager sees a pest map on the site layout, a trend line, the next vendor visit and a one-click audit pack. HQ sees the policy editor, approved-method list and cross-site patterns.
AI role. Photo classification suggestions and phrasing only. Chemical guidance is excluded from AI output.
Integration. Digital Site Map, Waste Age Clock, Leachate Loop, Odor Complaint Loop, weather ingestion, compliance calendar for vendor licences, and the Incident Room for severe infestations.
Build notes and guardrails. The approved-method list is controlled content reviewed by a pest-management expert and tagged EXPERT-REVIEW. Respect hospital sensitivities with stricter defaults and clear access rules. Keep photos free of people, support offline use and apply retention limits.
Measure and honest limits. Pest Pressure Index trends, complaint counts, time to bring a problem under control and trap counts. Counts are manual and subjective and photo suggestions are imperfect, so the index shows the level of confidence and the number of inspections behind it.
Priority. Strong, especially for hotels and hospitals. Four to five engineer-weeks.
Feature 8: Ergonomic Load and Heat-Stress Guard
Why it matters. Composting is hard physical work done in heat. Workers fork heavy wet material, carry buckets, and stand beside an active pit whose core is at fifty-five to sixty-five degrees, in an Indore summer that can pass forty-five degrees or in the humidity of the monsoon. Injuries such as back strain, heat exhaustion and exposure to gases and bioaerosols drive absenteeism and turnover, which then lowers quality and raises cost for the client. The master prompt includes safety checklists and a heat advisory. This feature measures cumulative physical load and heat exposure and uses them to schedule work that protects people. It is framed strictly as welfare and not as surveillance, and for hospital and hotel groups it is a visible expression of their duty of care.
Who uses it and when. Workers receive gentle prompts during the shift. Supervisors use it to plan and balance work. The manager sees aggregated risk. HQ owns the policies.
How it works. Each task type has an Effort Profile reviewed by an ergonomist: estimated kilograms handled (from load records), duration, posture category (bending, lifting, carrying, forking), walking distance from the site map and whether protective equipment adds heat load. The system converts these to Physical Load Units using a simple, transparent formula, accumulated per shift and per week. Heat exposure is computed from ambient temperature and humidity (a heat index) from the weather feed, plus a proximity factor when working near an active hot pit, the time spent outdoors and protective clothing. The result is a heat-stress band of low, moderate, high or extreme, using thresholds supplied by an occupational health expert and tagged EXPERT-REVIEW.
The Guard then acts in six ways. It feeds the planner (Volume 1 Feature 9) so heavy tasks fall in cooler hours, are rotated across workers, and stay inside a daily load budget. It inserts rest and hydration prompts with audio when the heat band is high, for example Dhoop tez hai, paani peeyein, and logs break taps for the supervisor without enforcing them. It shows the locations of water points and shade on the site map. It gives supervisors a weekly load balance view with kind suggestions such as consider lighter tasks tomorrow. It offers a symptom check-in using icons (dizzy, nauseous, very tired) which immediately informs the supervisor and displays first-aid instructions approved by an expert. And when load is persistently high it proposes equipment such as a trolley, a lighter fork or a shredder, as a Decision Inbox item with a cost and benefit estimate. Privacy is central: individual data is visible first to the worker, managers see aggregated team views unless the worker consents or safety demands otherwise, and the data is never used for discipline or pay.
Data model. EffortProfile by task type, WorkerShiftLoad (derived), HeatRiskSnapshot by site and time, BreakEvent, SymptomReport, and LoadBudgetPolicy with versions and approvals.
Screens and experience. The worker sees a simple Aaj ka bojh bar and a heat colour with large Aaram and Paani buttons. The manager sees a team load heat map in aggregate form and heat alerts for the site. HQ sees policy editors and fleet-level patterns.
AI role. None for the risk calculation. Gemini may phrase prompts and translate them.
Integration. Aaj ka Plan, weather ingestion, the Digital Site Map, safety checklists, the Incident Room, the Equipment register, and roster management.
Build notes and guardrails. Mark all thresholds and effort profiles EXPERT-REVIEW. Make participation opt-in for symptom reports, treat health-related data as sensitive under the privacy rules in C6, require no wearables, and do not offer medical diagnosis. Never use this data to rank or penalise workers, and state that in the worker-facing explanation.
Measure and honest limits. Heat-related incidents, injuries and near-misses, absenteeism, turnover and break adherence. The load is estimated from logged tasks rather than measured from the body, so the interface calls it an estimate and encourages workers to speak up when they feel unwell.
Priority. Strong, with differentiating value for welfare-conscious clients. Four to five engineer-weeks.
Part B: From pit to product
Feature 9: Curing, Screening and Bagging Line
Why it matters. When the hot, active phase ends, compost still has to cure and stabilise, be screened to remove oversize pieces and contaminants, and be stored and bagged. This is where product quality, yield and sellability are decided, yet at most facilities it is a heap in a corner. The pit sensors stop telling the story once material is moved, so the system loses track of it. A manager wants to know what is curing where, when it will be ready to screen, how much usable product came out, what the rejects were and where they went, how many bags exist and whether they are being stored properly.
Who uses it and when. Workers move, screen and bag material. Supervisors schedule and check. The manager approves release for sale or use. HQ maintains stage templates and defaults.
How it works. Define explicit stage gates for each batch: Active, Cure, Screen, Store and Bag, Dispatch. Curing areas become first-class Cure Piles with a QR label, location on the site map, link to the originating batch (Workstream L1), start date, weighed or estimated mass and dimensions. Because the Smart Spear stays in the pit, curing readiness uses a Maturity Check added to the Sense Check kit: temperature stable near ambient after turning, earthy smell, dark crumbly appearance, no reheating, and a simple germination test if the expert approves one, with each result recorded as icons. Readiness criteria are expert-defined and tagged EXPERT-REVIEW. The system forecasts the ready date using the maturity predictor (B5) and creates tasks: turn the cure pile occasionally, rewet if dry, cover it before rain through the Weather Playbooks, and schedule a screening session.
A Screening Session records the mesh size, input weight, fines, overs and contaminants by category (plastic, glass, stones, other), the equipment used from the register, the time taken and the number of workers. Yield is computed automatically. Overs can return to a pit as bulking material or inoculum and are then tracked by the Parat Planner as a recycled-overs material; contaminants go to a disposal log. Bagging records bag size (for example 5, 10, 25 and 50 kilograms), the target weight and spot-check weights using a verified scale (Feature 15), the number of bags, a lot label with a QR that leads to the batch passport (Volume 1 Feature 7), the storage location and conditions, and a shelf-life reminder with rewetting guidance. A first-in-first-out rule prompts the oldest lot first. Quality gates prevent mistakes: bagging is blocked until a moisture check is recorded within a set window (compost that is too wet can heat in bags), a contamination check is done and the manager approves release. A yield funnel shows how mass moves from load to cure, screen and bag, feeding the mass balance (Feature 10).
Data model. CurePile, CureCheck, ScreeningSession, ContaminantRecord, BagLot (lot ID, batch, bag size, count, weight checks, label ID, storage location, shelf-life date) and ReleaseApproval.
Screens and experience. The worker sees simple stage cards titled Pakne ke liye rakha, Chhanai and Bori bharai, with large buttons, QR scanning and a weight entry using the visual calculator. The manager sees a Finishing Line board with columns for Curing, Ready to screen, Screened, Bagged and Dispatched, with days in stage and bottleneck warnings. HQ sees stage templates and default criteria.
AI role. Gemini can suggest a quality category from a photo of screened product or flag visible contaminants for human confirmation, and can draft label text from approved templates. It makes no release decisions.
Integration. Batch management, the time-and-temperature certificate, mass balance, laboratory sampling (Feature 11), the manure formulator (Feature 12), the equipment register, the site map, the compost inventory in Workstream L2 and the day-close checklist.
Build notes and guardrails. Maturity criteria and moisture limits come from experts, and product labelling and any fertiliser-regulation requirements are LEGAL-REVIEW items. Scale verification must exist before bag weights are trusted. Support offline use at the yard, label printing through standard PDF sheets, and photo capture.
Measure and honest limits. Days in each stage, accuracy of the ready-date forecast, screening yield, contaminant share, bags per batch and storage heating incidents. Maturity decisions still need human and laboratory verification, and stage tracking is only as complete as the logging, which is why scanning makes it nearly effortless.
Priority. Must-win for facilities that sell or reuse compost, Strong otherwise. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Feature 10: Mass Balance and Loss Reconciliation
Why it matters. Hospital and hotel managers ask a pointed question: is all my waste really being composted, and where does the compost go? They also fear leakage, whether that is compost taken home, waste dumped elsewhere or simple mis-weighing. Carbon and sustainability claims, diversion certificates and the ROI tracker all rest on mass numbers, yet today mass is logged piecemeal in the diary, loads, bagging and dispatch with no reconciliation between them. A mass-balance ledger that follows the material from the kitchen door to the buyer, and that states plainly how much is measured versus estimated, makes the numbers defensible and exposes anomalies early.
Who uses it and when. Managers review it weekly and monthly. Auditors and finance staff use it for sustainability and ROI reporting. Supervisors use the data-quality suggestions. HQ compares conversion ratios across the fleet.
How it works. For each facility define a flow graph. Nodes are sources (kitchen, banquet, garden), holding points, pits and batches, cure piles, screening, bag stock, buyers or internal use, and disposal. Edges are movements recorded with mass, estimated moisture, source of the number (weighed, estimated from container calibration, derived) and the event that produced them: the intake diary, load records, bulking material use, water events, leachate events, screening sessions, bagging, and sales or dispatch. Reconciliation runs per batch and per period. It computes the input mass, applies expert-defined loss factor ranges by waste type and phase (composting reduces mass and volume substantially, mostly through water and carbon loss, so expected output is a range and not a point), and compares the expected output range with the recorded output. The unexplained variance is classified as within the expected range, lower than expected (possible leakage, unrecorded use or overestimated input) or higher than expected (underestimated input or unrecorded additions).
The system is honest about data quality. Each movement carries a measured-or-estimated flag, the reconciliation reports the share of mass that was actually weighed, and the evidence strength score (Volume 1 Feature 6) reflects it. It suggests how to improve, for example weigh three representative buckets each week or calibrate the scale. Anomaly rules raise flags such as output below expectation for three consecutive batches, or a mismatch between ledger stock and a physical count; a periodic Stock Count mode lets a worker scan bag lots and cure piles to confirm what exists. A Where did my kilograms go view draws a flow diagram for a batch or a month, with losses labelled as water vapour, carbon loss, leachate, rejects and unexplained. Diversion totals used by sustainability reports (Workstream L3) come from the reconciled numbers with uncertainty ranges, and if reconciliation is poor the interface labels them Estimated with low confidence. The tone of every finding is neutral: the system never accuses anyone, it raises a question for the manager to resolve.
Data model. MassMovement (from, to, kilograms, moisture estimate, source type, event reference, time, confidence), LossFactorProfile by waste type and phase with expert approval, ReconciliationRun (scope, period, inputs, expected range, actual, variance, status), StockCount and MassAnomaly.
Screens and experience. The manager sees a Mass Balance page with a flow diagram, variance cards, the share of measured mass and a list of data-quality fixes. Auditors see a read-only version tied to sealed records. HQ sees an editor for loss factors and anonymised conversion ratios across the fleet.
AI role. Gemini narrates the findings in plain language from the computed numbers. It produces no figures.
Integration. The intake ledger (Workstream M1), the Parat Planner, Water Budget, Leachate Loop, the finishing line, offtake, compost inventory, sustainability accounting (L3), the evidence chain and evidence strength features from Volume 1.
Build notes and guardrails. Use ranges everywhere and avoid false precision. Loss factors are expert-defined and can be refined from a facility’s own history. Treat any diversion or carbon claims as LEGAL-REVIEW. Use rollups so reconciliation is fast on large histories, and make findings wording non-accusatory.
Measure and honest limits. Share of batches reconciling within range, unexplained variance, stock-count accuracy and the share of mass measured rather than estimated. The system can flag that numbers do not add up; it cannot prove theft or identify who is responsible, and evaporation and moisture variation limit precision.
Priority. Must-win for trust, sustainability reporting and ROI integrity. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Feature 11: Sampling, Lab Chain-of-Custody and Grading
Why it matters. Selling or using compost requires credible quality data. Laboratory tests of moisture, pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, maturity indices and, in some contexts, heavy metals remain the gold standard, and sensors cannot replace them. But facilities handle samples informally: labels get lost, a single scoop is taken as a sample, results arrive as a PDF in an email and are never linked to the batch or trended. Linking laboratory results to batch records and process data gives the best of both worlds, because it supports grading and marketing, and it calibrates the system’s own models.
Who uses it and when. Supervisors and technicians take samples. Managers review results and approve grades. Partner laboratories upload results through a limited portal. Buyers can view a quality certificate. HQ maintains grade profiles.
How it works. A Sampling Plan per batch states when to sample (at maturity, before bagging, periodically), how many sub-samples to take, from which locations and depths with a picture-based guide (for example the quartering method), how to prepare a composite and what container to use. In the app, the supervisor scans the batch QR, follows the plan, photographs the sub-samples and creates a Sample with a unique QR label. Chain of custody records each step with timestamps and the responsible person: collected, stored, handed to a courier or the lab (confirmed by tap or PIN), received by the lab (confirmed by scanning), results uploaded and reviewed. Each step is sealed with the evidence chain (Volume 1 Feature 5), so the path of a sample is verifiable.
The Lab Partner Portal is a limited role that shows only sample IDs and requested tests and lets the lab upload structured results or a report file. Structured results record each parameter with units, method, detection limits and the lab’s accreditation reference, validated for plausible ranges and unit conversions. Grading uses Grade Profiles configured by an expert and a regulatory reviewer, because requirements differ by standard and jurisdiction; the agent must not hard-code parameters and must tag every profile LEGAL-REVIEW. A profile maps results to outcomes such as Grade A, Grade B, Needs reprocessing or Not for sale, and to fit-for-use categories such as ornamental use or edible crops. Trends compare batches over time and relate quality to process data such as time-and-temperature exposure, turning count and mix quality, which tells HQ what drives quality.
Laboratory data feeds the system’s own accuracy. Measured moisture, pH and carbon-to-nitrogen values update the local material overrides in the Parat Planner, calibrate virtual sensors, and trigger a calibration review through Sense Check if the laboratory and the sensor disagree about end-of-batch values. An out-of-specification result opens a Decision Inbox item with options: re-cure, re-screen, blend with other compost, or reject. The system produces a Batch Quality Certificate combining laboratory results, process evidence and seals, linked from bag QR labels. It also tracks cost per batch and recommends a sampling frequency that balances cost and risk.
Data model. SamplingPlan, Sample (ID, batch, sub-samples, composite, steps), CustodyEvent, LabReport (lab, accreditation reference, method, results, file), GradeProfile with versions and approvals, GradeOutcome and RetestDecision.
Screens and experience. The supervisor sees a guided sampling flow with pictures. The manager sees a Quality page with grade, trend, cost and certificate. The lab sees a simple upload form. HQ sees a grade-profile editor with an approval workflow and a lab directory.
AI role. Gemini extracts parameters from uploaded lab reports (PDF or photo) into a draft structured form with confidence per field, and a human confirms before saving. It can also draft a plain-language summary for buyers from approved wording.
Integration. Batch management, the Safe Compost Proof from Volume 1, the Parat Planner learning loop, virtual sensors, the evidence chain, the formulator, the offtake planner and the compliance calendar.
Build notes and guardrails. Never describe a result as certified unless the laboratory is accredited and the reference is recorded. Keep chain-of-custody records tamper-evident. Support offline sampling and multiple laboratories, and convert units carefully. Buyer-facing documents show only what the client approves.
Measure and honest limits. Share of batches sampled per plan, laboratory turnaround time, grade distribution, laboratory versus sensor discrepancy and cost per batch. A laboratory result is a snapshot of one composite sample and cannot represent every part of a pile, and the certificate says so.
Priority. Strong, and Must-win for facilities that sell compost. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Feature 12: Value-Added Manure Formulator and Production Orders
Why it matters. Plain compost sells cheaply. Value-added products such as enriched manure, potting mix, vermicompost blends, neem-enriched manure and liquid products can sell at a multiple of that price, and they create repeat customers among nurseries, terrace gardeners, landscapers and the hotels’ own gardening teams. But formulation is done by guesswork, nobody knows the true cost and margin, recipes are not repeatable, and there is no production order, no consistent label and no record of what went into which bag. A facility with surplus compost that cannot turn it into revenue weakens the return-on-investment story for your own subscription, so a formulation and production tool serves both the client and you.
Who uses it and when. Managers and supervisors plan production. Workers mix and bag. Sales staff use the price lists. HQ product specialists maintain template recipes and approvals.
How it works. A Product Catalogue lists SKUs such as Garden Compost 5 kg, Potting Mix 10 kg or Neem-Enriched Manure 25 kg. Each product has a specification (target ranges for moisture, pH and conductivity), the eligible grades of base compost, an ingredient list with proportion ranges (compost lots, cocopeat, sand, neem cake, vermicompost, biochar or other approved inputs), bag sizes, a label template and a shelf life. In the Formulator the user picks a product and a target quantity. The system calculates the requirement for each ingredient, selects eligible compost lots by grade, age and laboratory results, checks stock in the compost and ingredient inventories, predicts the blend’s parameters as a weighted mix with uncertainty and warns when the prediction falls outside the specification. It also reports cost per kilogram and margin against the price list.
A Formulation Optimiser uses deterministic linear optimisation over available lots and ingredients to find the lowest-cost blend that meets the specification, and it explains why it chose that blend. A Production Order captures the planned date, quantity, assigned workers and steps (mix, rest, screen, bag, label), records which lots were consumed, the yield, quality checks (moisture, smell, contamination) and the manager’s release approval. Traceability is central: every finished lot links to the blend lot, the compost batches, the pits and the source dates, so if a batch later proves problematic the system produces a recall report listing every product that contains it. Label sheets are generated as PDFs with product name, net weight, lot, date, ingredients, usage instructions from approved text and a QR leading to the batch passport. Regulatory fields on labels are placeholders tagged LEGAL-REVIEW, since selling organic manure or fertiliser may require registration, specific labelling or nutrient declarations that the agent must not assume.
Data model. Product, ProductSpec, Recipe with versioned ranges, IngredientLot, ProductionOrder, FinishedLot, GenealogyLink, QCCheck, PriceList and LabelTemplate.
Screens and experience. The manager sees a catalogue, a Formulator with specification meters and cost, and an order board. Workers follow production-order steps with large buttons and a reuse of the mix-meter interface from the Parat Planner. HQ sees recipe templates with an approval workflow.
AI role. Gemini drafts product descriptions and usage text in several languages from approved attributes, and the manager reviews every claim against the specification before publishing. It makes no formulation decisions.
Integration. Compost inventory (Workstream L2), laboratory results (Feature 11), the finishing line, the Parat Planner interface, offtake planning, the equipment register, the evidence chain and the invoicing module.
Build notes and guardrails. Fertiliser regulation, labelling, nutrient claims, registration and GST treatment are LEGAL-REVIEW items, and recipes are EXPERT-REVIEW items. Never state a nutrient content unless it comes from laboratory data. Include handling safety notes for ingredients such as neem cake or rock phosphate, and keep traceability immutable once a lot is released.
Measure and honest limits. Margin per kilogram, share of compost sold as value-added products, production yield, quality-check pass rate and recall readiness. Predicted blend parameters are estimates, and demand for each product is uncertain until sold.
Priority. Strong where facilities have surplus compost, otherwise Opportunistic. Five to seven engineer-weeks.
Feature 13: Offtake Planner and Revenue Forecast
Why it matters. Many facilities produce compost they cannot sell on time. Piles accumulate, space runs out, quality degrades or the compost is given away or dumped. Buyers such as nurseries, farmers, landscapers, municipal gardens and terrace gardeners have seasonal demand, such as sowing seasons and the planting period after the monsoon, and they want predictable supply. Without planning the facility misses revenue and blocks its own space, which undermines the return-on-investment story. The inventory and marketplace work in the master prompt record stock but do not plan supply against demand.
Who uses it and when. The manager or a sales coordinator uses it weekly. Workers handle dispatch tasks. Buyers use a simple link to see availability and place enquiries. HQ commercial staff oversee the curated marketplace layer.
How it works. The Supply Forecast projects how much compost of which grade will be ready in each of the coming weeks, drawn from batches in the curing line and the maturity forecasts, shown as ranges, for example 1.2 to 1.5 tonnes of Grade A over the next eight weeks. The Demand side starts from a buyer directory: buyer type, crops, typical volumes, frequency, location and preferred pack sizes, plus enquiries and past orders. A Demand Calendar of regional planting seasons, editable by an agronomist or commercial lead and marked for verification, adds seasonality. A simple forecast combines history and calendar.
Matching allocates expected supply to buyers and orders using rules (grade required, distance, price) and proposes pre-sell offers, such as reserving 500 kilograms for a particular nursery by a given date. Order management covers quotes, confirmations, dispatch scheduling, delivery slips, weight verification at dispatch on a verified scale, proof of delivery by photo or tap, invoices through the invoicing module (GST rules are LEGAL-REVIEW), payment tracking with reminders, and complaints or returns. Capacity-driven nudges operate when cure space is nearly full and forecast supply exceeds demand: the system suggests a discount offer, conversion to a value-added product (Feature 12), or a donation to a municipal garden recorded for sustainability reporting (tax treatment is LEGAL-REVIEW). A Revenue Forecast gives scenarios for price and volume and a cash timeline, and a Price Pulse view tracks realised prices by product and buyer type against market prices entered manually. Standing supply agreements track committed monthly quantities and fulfilment. A public enquiry form on the Impact Page (Volume 1 Feature 23) feeds new buyers into the directory.
Data model. Buyer and BuyerProfile, Enquiry, Quote, OfftakeOrder, Reservation, DispatchRecord with weights and proof, Payment, SupplyForecast, DemandCalendar, PriceObservation and StandingAgreement.
Screens and experience. The manager sees a Bikri board with supply versus demand by week, orders, reservations, a dispatch calendar and money due. A dispatch worker sees tasks with weight entry and proof of delivery. A buyer link shows availability, certificates and an enquiry form. HQ sees marketplace oversight.
AI role. Gemini drafts offer messages in Hindi or English from structured data for the manager to send, and extracts order details from a pasted WhatsApp message into a draft for confirmation. It makes no pricing decisions.
Integration. The finishing line, grading, the formulator, compost inventory, mass balance (dispatch reduces stock), the batch passport and evidence chain, invoicing, the Green Badge page and the Decision Inbox for offer and discount approvals.
Build notes and guardrails. Treat GST, e-invoicing, sale of organic manure, contracts and donations as LEGAL-REVIEW items. Use a payment provider and never store card data. Keep buyer data private and obtain consent for marketing messages. Market price inputs are manual or from licensed sources and never scraped. Never publish prices automatically.
Measure and honest limits. Sell-through time, share of production sold, price realisation, days of stock, revenue per tonne and dispatch accuracy. Demand forecasts are noisy and depend on buyer data that starts thin, so early versions are mostly a well-organised order book with forecasts that improve with use.
Priority. Strong where facilities sell compost. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Part C: Management pages and workflows
Feature 14: Digital Site Map and Zone Manager
Why it matters. A great many features need to know where things are: where each pit, node, bin, tap, drain and hazard sits, where shade and water points are, which routes workers walk, where sensitive areas such as wards, restaurants or guest rooms lie in relation to the compost yard, and where an inspector should go. Today the system may store only a latitude and longitude for each pit. A facility-scale digital map that a manager can build in minutes becomes a foundation for planning, safety, installation and reporting, and it makes the platform feel like an operating system for the yard.
Who uses it and when. The manager or supervisor creates and edits it during onboarding and when the yard changes. Technicians use it during installation. Workers view it daily. Inspectors see it during visits. HQ keeps templates.
How it works. The user uploads a site photo, a satellite screenshot or a floor plan (image or PDF), or draws a schematic on a canvas. Scale is set by marking two points and entering the real distance between them, and optionally the map is georeferenced with two or three GPS points for outdoor sites. The user drags icons onto the map: pits, cure piles, nodes linked to their asset records, holding bins, scales, water points and hoses, drains, sumps and tanks, shredders and other equipment, storage areas, gates, hazard zones (no-entry, confined-space), PPE stations, first-aid and fire-extinguisher points, shade, handwash and toilets, and sensitive zones. The user also draws walking routes and named zones (polygons) with properties, and sets a wind-direction reference. Layers can be switched on and off, and every change is versioned and audited.
The map is a graph, so the system can compute walking distances and times between points. These numbers feed Aaj ka Plan, the collection round planner, the ergonomic load estimates and incident response. Instead of continuous location tracking, workers scan zone QR plates to say where they are, which is privacy-friendly and works indoors. Zone Manager assigns each zone an owner and rules: a pit area adjoining a ward gets stricter pest thresholds, a hazard zone restricts which tasks can be assigned, and a hot-weather zone may require shade breaks. Overlays colour the map by pit health, pest pressure, odor risk, container waste age and water-point status, with a time slider to replay the past. Printable outputs include a site safety poster with evacuation routes and QR plate positions, and inspectors get a map view with Pit Passport links. During onboarding a setup wizard makes the first usable map in about ten minutes.
Data model. SiteMap (image reference, scale, georeference), MapItem (type, entity reference, position, rotation), MapZone (polygon, properties, owner), MapPath, Hazard and MapVersion.
Screens and experience. The manager edits on a canvas with drag-and-drop and layers. The worker sees a simple Nakshaa view with large icons and a highlighted path to the next task. Technicians place nodes during installation. HQ sees templates.
AI role. Gemini can propose item placements from a site photo and a short description and can describe a route as audio in Hindi, with the user confirming any placement. Nothing safety-critical depends on it.
Integration. Aaj ka Plan, the Waste Age Clock round planner, water points, the Leachate Loop, the Pest workflow, the Ergonomic Guard, the odor map, the Incident Room, the equipment register, the onboarding wizard and inspector views.
Build notes and guardrails. Support large images with tiling and low-memory phones using SVG or canvas wisely. Provide accessible alternative text and a list view of items. The map is an operational aid and not an official fire-safety or evacuation plan, which is a LEGAL-REVIEW matter, and the interface must say so. Do not rely on GPS indoors and do not track workers.
Measure and honest limits. Share of sites with a completed map, planning time saved, incident response times and planner accuracy. Map accuracy depends on the user and is not survey-grade, so distances are described as approximate.
Priority. Strong, as a foundation for many other features. Four to five engineer-weeks.
Feature 15: Tools, Equipment and Scale Calibration Register
Why it matters. Composting depends on physical tools: forks, wheelbarrows, buckets, hoses, shredders, sieves, mixers, thermometers, moisture meters, weighing scales, tarpaulins, pumps and protective equipment. A broken fork or missing hose means a skipped task, and nobody records the reason. Most importantly, an uncalibrated weighing scale quietly corrupts every mass number in the system: the intake diary, the mass balance, sustainability reports, the ROI tracker and compost sales. That is the hidden weak point in the data, and an auditor who asks when the scale was last verified will find that nobody knows. Facilities also lack records of shredder downtime, maintenance and personal protective equipment stock, so shortages appear in the middle of a shift.
Who uses it and when. Workers check tools in and out and report faults. Supervisors schedule maintenance. Managers approve spending. Vendors and technicians record calibration and repairs. HQ maintains templates and sees fleet patterns.
How it works. Create an equipment register for facility tools, separate from the node assets in Physical_Assets. Each item has a QR tag, type, make and model, serial number, location on the site map, condition, owning zone, purchase date and cost, supplier and warranty. Shared tools can be checked out and returned by scanning the tag, and a missing-tool alert appears if something is not returned by the end of the day (recorded for follow-up, never to charge workers). A fault report is an icon plus a photo or voice note, labelled Kharab hai. It creates a maintenance ticket and, where the item is required by a task, tells Aaj ka Plan to pick an alternative or flag the gap. Preventive maintenance plans are defined by equipment type and usage: shredder blade sharpening, belts and oil, hose replacement. Usage hours come from logs such as screening sessions. The system also tracks vendor contracts, spare parts, downtime and cost, and proposes replacement through the Decision Inbox when repair cost and downtime cross a threshold.
Scales and measuring devices form a special class. Each has a calibration schedule and a quick-check routine: daily or weekly the worker weighs a known reference weight (or a standard jug of water) and enters the displayed value with the visual calculator, and the system compares it with the expected value within a configurable tolerance. The result sets a status of In tolerance, Drift warning or Out of service. Formal periodic verification by an authorised agency, including any requirements under legal metrology rules for weighing devices used in trade, is tracked as due dates and certificates, tagged LEGAL-REVIEW, and the system never asserts what is legally required. This status is linked into the data. Every mass entry records which scale produced it, and when a scale is out of tolerance or overdue the measured masses are downgraded to estimated in the evidence strength score and the mass balance, and flagged for review. The Balti ka wazan container calibrations are tracked the same way, as are thermometers, moisture meters and pH pens used for Sense Check, with their buffer solutions and expiry dates. PPE stock is tracked per item and size with issue records and reorder alerts, and a task that needs gloves shows a warning if none are in stock.
Data model. EquipmentAsset and EquipmentType, ToolCheckout, FaultReport, MaintenancePlan and MaintenanceEvent, ScaleCalibrationCheck, ReferenceWeight with its own certificate, VerificationCertificate, PPEStock and PPEIssue.
Screens and experience. The worker sees an Auzaar list with scan-to-take and scan-to-return and a Kharab hai button, plus a daily Aaj ka wazan check card. The manager sees an equipment board with status colours, downtime, upcoming maintenance and a prominent banner whenever a scale is out of tolerance. HQ sees templates and fleet-level equipment patterns.
AI role. Gemini can classify a fault from a photo or voice note and draft a maintenance ticket for the user to confirm. It does no calibration decisions.
Integration. The site map, Aaj ka Plan, mass balance, evidence strength, the Parat Planner container calibration, the finishing line (shredder and sieve usage), the Day Close checklist, the compliance calendar for verification due dates, the Decision Inbox and the Ergonomic Guard.
Build notes and guardrails. Reference weights need their own traceable records. Tolerances are configurable and expert-approved. Link historical mass records to the scale status in force at the time, so history remains explainable. Make daily checks fast and offline-capable. Keep wording about lost or damaged tools neutral.
Measure and honest limits. Share of scales in tolerance, share of mass from verified scales, tool downtime and tasks skipped because of tools. Checks are manual, and a reference weight is only as good as its own accuracy.
Priority. Must-win for data integrity (the scale portion), Strong overall. Four to five engineer-weeks.
Feature 16: SOP Studio with Versioned Adherence
Why it matters. Every facility has a way of doing things, but it lives in people’s heads or in a document nobody reads. Enterprise clients, especially hospitals and hotel chains, need controlled, written procedures for internal audits and quality systems, inspectors ask to see them, and new workers need them to learn. The task-card builder defines buttons and the policy templates in Volume 1 push group settings, but nothing authors, version-controls, teaches and measures the standard operating procedures themselves.
Who uses it and when. Managers, supervisors and HQ agronomists author. Workers follow. Auditors review. Group administrators distribute templates.
How it works. In the SOP Studio a user creates an SOP with a title, purpose, scope (pit profiles, roles and zones), and steps. Each step has text, pictures, an optional short audio or video clip, safety notes, checks, required tools, PPE and materials (linked to the equipment register and material library), expected evidence (a sensor signature, a photo or a scan) and a time estimate. Branches allow if-then flows such as if the smell is sharp, go to SOP 7. References to regulations are allowed and tagged LEGAL-REVIEW. HQ provides a library of expert-reviewed base SOPs for loading, turning, watering, curing, screening, sampling, pest control, leachate handling, PPE and emergencies; a facility adopts one and customises it, and the studio shows a comparison against the base.
Each SOP moves through Draft, Review, Approved, Effective and Superseded states, with approvals by defined roles (two steps for safety-critical SOPs), a change summary and a locked history. The system can answer which version was in force on any date. Hindi, Hinglish and English versions are managed together: Gemini drafts a simplified, worker-level translation and an audio script, and the version stays NEEDS-NATIVE-REVIEW until a fluent person approves it. Workers see SOP content inside their tasks, for example the turning SOP content powers the Palatna Coach and the loading SOP powers the Parat Planner, with an SOP dekhein button and offline caching.
When a new version is approved, affected users receive a short briefing (audio plus a tap to say samajh gaya or a three-question quiz), and the manager sees who has acknowledged it, without punitive framing. Adherence links SOP steps to the evidence produced by the coach, the planner and the other tools, producing a summary of how often key steps were followed (PPE ticked, pit covered after loading) and a list of deviation categories with the reasons workers chose. Group dashboards show adherence to group templates. An Audit Pack bundles the SOPs in force on a date, acknowledgements and adherence, sealed as a document. A change impact view lists which task cards, coaching profiles and checklists are affected by an SOP change and proposes updates. Workers can flag a step that does not work, with a voice note, which enters an improvement queue linked to Ustaad Mode (Volume 1 Feature 14), and the system reminds owners of annual reviews.

Screens and experience. The manager edits in blocks with a phone-frame preview and sees an approvals inbox and an adherence dashboard. The worker sees step cards with pictures and audio. HQ sees a template library with differences against facility copies.
AI role. Gemini drafts steps from a manager’s rough notes or voice description, simplifies language, proposes picture lists, drafts translations and audio scripts. All of it is draft until a person approves, and it never publishes anything.
Integration. The task-card builder, Palatna Coach, Parat Planner, Water Assistant, the equipment register, group policy templates, the compliance catalogue (mapping SOPs to requirements), inspection mode, the Skill Passport and the Decision Inbox for approvals.
Build notes and guardrails. Treat approved versions as immutable and audited. Make step cards work offline and accessible. Regulatory references are LEGAL-REVIEW. Make clear that adherence to an SOP is an operational record and is not by itself proof of regulatory compliance.
Measure and honest limits. Share of SOPs approved and current, acknowledgement rate, adherence on measurable steps, time to roll out a change and audit preparation time. Adherence is measured only where evidence exists, and the quality of the SOPs depends on their authors.

Feature 17: Day Close and Variance Review
Why it matters. At the end of a shift things are forgotten: a lid left open, weights not logged, materials not counted, tools not returned, verifications still pending. Managers discover the gaps next morning or during an audit. A short, structured end-of-day ritual closes the loop, gives the manager a signed daily record and surfaces variances while the shift’s memory is fresh. The Silent-Failure Watchdog in Volume 1 detects absence algorithmically; Day Close is the human routine and the artefact it produces.
Who uses it and when. The shift lead or supervisor completes it before leaving. The manager reviews and signs. Auditors can view the history. HQ maintains templates.
How it works. A Day Close Checklist is generated for each facility and shift from the modules the facility actually uses. Pits: covers closed and tasks planned versus done, with exceptions pulled from the plan and the ledger. Pending Kaam Ka Saboot items still in Checking. Weights: intake totals against forecast or banquet events. Containers: open items on the Waste Age Clock and any over their limit. Materials: quick counts for items below the reorder point. Water and leachate: tank level checks. Pests: new observations. Equipment: tools still checked out, faults reported, the scale daily check, PPE stock. Safety: incidents or near-misses, heat breaks logged. Site: gates locked and hazards cleared. Finally a structured handover note for the next shift: what is pending, which pits to watch, and any tips.
Where data already exists the item auto-fills with a green tick and its source; where it does not, the app asks a single tap, Haan, Nahi or Lagu nahi, and asks for a reason (with an optional photo) when the answer is Nahi. The Variance Review computes the top few variances, such as planned versus actual tasks, intake versus forecast, ledger versus physical counts and unusual timings, and asks for a short explanation by voice or icon. The lead signs with a tap or PIN, the manager reviews the same night or next morning to accept, ask for clarification or create follow-up tasks, and both sign-offs are sealed with the evidence chain. The Day Close Report is a one-page PDF. If a close is late or missing, the watchdog raises a gentle reminder and then an escalation. In multi-shift facilities the next shift must acknowledge the handover before starting, as a soft requirement. Trends show completeness, common variance causes and links to incidents. HQ and group templates set the base checklist, and facilities adjust items through the SOP Studio. A worker can dictate the handover, and Gemini turns the voice note into a structured draft for confirmation.
Data model. DayCloseTemplate, DayCloseRecord (facility, date, shift, items with status, source, reason and photo, variances, handover, signatures), Variance and FollowUpTask.
Screens and experience. The lead sees a Day Close card at the end of the shift with an auto-filled list and big Haan and Nahi buttons. The manager sees a calendar of days coloured by completeness, open variances and a sign button. HQ sees templates and fleet completion rates.
AI role. Gemini structures a voice handover and suggests wording for variance explanations; it makes no decisions.
Integration. Aaj ka Plan, the Waste Age Clock, the equipment register, mass balance, water and leachate, the pest workflow, the Ergonomic Guard, the watchdog, the Decision Inbox, the evidence chain and the Report Studio.
Build notes and guardrails. Keep the close under five minutes by auto-filling as much as possible and showing only relevant items. Support offline use. Keep reasons non-punitive and let managers tune strictness.
Measure and honest limits. Close-out completion rate, time to complete, variances resolved and incidents caught early. It depends on honest answers, so occasional photo spot-checks are optional, and the system does not claim to detect dishonesty.
Priority. Strong. Three to four engineer-weeks.
Feature 18: Correction Requests and the Data Coverage Calendar
Why it matters. Real data entry is imperfect. A worker forgets to log yesterday’s weights, types a wrong quantity, logs a load against the wrong pit, creates a duplicate, or a roster changes after the fact. Today the options are either rigid (edits blocked outside a back-dating window) or loose (a manager or HQ editor changes records through the data grid, silently). The first frustrates people into not logging at all; the second destroys the audit trust that the whole product is built on. A controlled maker-and-checker correction process keeps data honest without punishing honest mistakes, and a coverage calendar shows managers where the gaps are before an inspector or a report finds them.
Who uses it and when. Workers and supervisors request corrections. Managers approve. Auditors can view the amendment log. HQ sets the rules and uses the same mechanism for its own edits.
How it works. A Correction Request has a type (add a missing entry, change quantity or unit, change pit, material or time, void a duplicate, reassign a worker, mark a task as not done or done with a reason), the proposed new values, a reason given by icon or voice note, and optional evidence such as a photo. Rules depend on the data type and age. Within a manager-defined window (for example twenty-four hours) the same worker can correct low-risk entries and the change is logged. Beyond that window a manager must approve. Beyond a longer limit the correction needs a written reason and is marked in reports as a late amendment. Compliance-critical records such as verified claims, calibrations and sealed documents are never edited in place: the system creates an amendment record that links to the original, and some require two approvals. The requester can never approve their own request.
Approved corrections create a new version with full history, the original remains visible, and the evidence chain seals the amendment. The system then runs the downstream recalculation for mass balance, engines and the compliance matrix, and tells the manager what changed, for example this correction changes the March diversion total by twelve kilograms and the March report is now superseded, regenerate? Late amendments reduce the evidence strength slightly with a visible explanation. Pattern detection looks for repeated late logging by a person or shift and recommends a process fix (training, easier logging) rather than blame.
The Data Coverage Calendar is a heat map with rows for each pit and data stream (telemetry, tasks, weights, water, leachate, Sense Checks, calibrations, Day Close) and columns for days. Each cell is complete, partial, missing or not applicable. Clicking a cell shows what is missing and offers one tap to log now or request a correction. A user can mark a day Not applicable with a reason and approval, for example a pit under maintenance or a festival closure, so that false gaps do not appear. An Audit Readiness mode takes a chosen period and shows gaps against the compliance matrix requirements, ranked by importance, and exports a coverage statement. A weekly note tells the manager the coverage percentage and the biggest gaps.
Data model. CorrectionRequest (target reference, type, proposed values, reason, evidence, status, requester, approvers, times), RecordVersion, AmendmentFlag, CoverageCell (derived), NotApplicableMark and CorrectionPolicy.
Screens and experience. The worker long-presses any entry and chooses Galti sudhaar, a simple form with large buttons. The manager sees a Corrections inbox with a before-and-after view and the downstream impact, and a Coverage Calendar page. HQ edits the policy. Auditors see the amendment log.
AI role. Gemini transcribes voice reasons and summarises a batch of requests for the manager. It never approves anything.
Integration. The Khad-Kachra diary editing rules (Workstream E5), the audit log, the evidence chain, mass balance, the compliance matrix, Day Close, the watchdog, the Report Studio, and the HQ data grid, which must use the same versioning and amendment mechanism so that HQ edits are never invisible.
Build notes and guardrails. Make recalculation jobs idempotent, enforce permissions centrally, queue requests offline, mask personal data in shared views and apply the same rules to every edit path in the system.
Measure and honest limits. Coverage percentage, correction rate by type, approval turnaround and the share of late amendments. The system cannot verify that a correction is truthful, although evidence and patterns help, and coverage is not the same as quality, since data can be present and wrong.
Priority. Strong, because audit integrity depends on it. Four to five engineer-weeks.

Why it matters. Teams coordinate through scattered WhatsApp groups. A message about pit 3 gets lost among others, is never linked to the data, leaves no record for an audit, and faces a language barrier: the manager writes English, the worker reads Hindi, and the supervisor mixes both. Decisions and useful tips vanish. A conversation attached to the exact pit, alert, task claim, batch, sample, container, equipment fault or decision keeps context, creates an auditable record and, with instant translation and voice notes, removes the language and literacy barrier.
Who uses it and when. Every role, whenever a question or instruction concerns a specific item. HQ support can join with permission. Auditors can read.
How it works. Every entity page has a thread. A thread holds messages, voice notes of up to a minute, photos, @mentions of people or roles and pinned decisions. Each reader sees messages in their preferred language, with a control to view the original. Feature 22: Node Care Rounds, Client-Side Light Maintenance
Why it matters. Field visits to Smart Spear nodes are the largest operating cost of a hardware subscription at Rs. 750 per node per month, and many failures are mundane. The lens is fouled, compost is caked on the heatsink fins and reduces energy harvest, a cable gland is loose, the node has been nudged by a turning fork, the lid is not seated, ants have entered, or condensation has formed. Client staff are on site every day. With simple guided checks they can prevent or resolve many of these issues, which lowers truck rolls, improves uptime and protects the thermal harvesting efficiency that the whole battery-less design depends on. The principle is look, clean and report; it never involves opening the enclosure or touching electronics.
Who uses it and when. The Safai Mitra does a short weekly round. Supervisors see completion. Technicians and HQ operations guide targeted requests remotely.
How it works. A Care Routine template exists for each node model and firmware, with pictures. A weekly three-minute checklist asks whether the node is upright and not buried, whether the fins are free of compost (clean with a soft brush), whether the lens is clean (wipe with the supplied cloth), whether the lid and cable glands look and feel secure by hand without tools, whether the cable looks undamaged, and whether the buzzer sounds when the cloud sends a buzzer test through the command queue, which the worker confirms by tap. Each item records a result and, for key items, a photo. The system then measures what happened. After fin cleaning it compares the harvest rate and supercapacitor charging before and after, and the worker sees positive feedback such as Safai ke baad energy 18 percent behtar, computed from real data. After lens cleaning it compares image quality. Care compliance is tracked per node, not as a ranking of workers.
Remote guided diagnostics extend this. When the system detects a problem, such as a harvest drop, a dark image or weak signal, it sends a targeted Node Care Request to the site with two to four steps and the expected outcome. After the worker acts, the system forces a reading to verify. If fixed, the request closes and a truck roll is avoided; if not, the system schedules a technician with a bundle of information about what has already been tried. Gemini can look at a worker’s photo of the node and suggest signs of dirt, displacement or possible water ingress for confirmation. Stop-and-report triggers are explicit: a burning smell, a swollen enclosure or water inside means stop and call. Consumables such as lens cloths and brushes are tracked in the equipment register, and completing care rounds earns credit toward the Skill Passport. HQ sees a truck-roll avoidance dashboard showing visits avoided, uptime improvement and harvest efficiency. Because customers are performing limited actions on company-owned hardware, the instruction set and the warranty or liability terms are reviewed and tagged LEGAL-REVIEW.
Data model. CareRoutineTemplate, CareRound (node, worker, items, photos, time), CareRequest (node, trigger, steps, status), CareOutcome (pre and post metrics, resolved flag) and DamageReport.
Screens and experience. The worker sees a Node ki dekhbhal card from Aaj ka Plan each week with big photo steps and tick buttons. The manager sees a Node Care board of due, done and flagged items. HQ sees the truck-roll avoidance dashboard.
AI role. Photo analysis as a suggestion and phrasing of instructions. It never gives electrical advice.
Integration. The Sensor Trust Score, node health (Workstream D2), the Wake Scheduler and command queue (Volume 1 Feature 1 and D4), the equipment register, Aaj ka Plan, the Skill Passport, the Decision Inbox for repeat problems, the Incident Room and safety guidance.
Build notes and guardrails. Restrict steps to non-invasive actions and have the hardware engineer approve every instruction and picture for each node model. Keep photos free of people, support offline use and keep records of who did what.
Measure and honest limits. Truck rolls per hundred nodes, care round completion, harvest improvement after cleaning and node uptime. Results depend on worker attention, and some faults still need a technician.
Priority. Must-win for unit economics. Four to five engineer-weeks.

Next to every number sits a small How is this calculated? link that opens the definition, the version, the inputs and the data freshness. Metrics are versioned with effective dates and impact notes (for example version 2 excludes Inconclusive verdicts), with options to backfill.
The Report Studio lets a user choose metrics, dimensions (time, facility, pit, pit profile, material, worker group in aggregate), filters and comparisons (period versus period, facility versus facility), choose a visualisation (table, line, bar or heat map, following the shared chart standards) and add narrative blocks. Templates cover monthly operations, sustainability, quality, labour and financial views. Reports can be scheduled and distributed by email, WhatsApp or a link, and each recipient only sees the data their permissions allow. Exports to CSV or sealed PDF automatically include an appendix of metric definitions. Links are read-only and expire. A Questions panel accepts a natural-language request such as show waste per occupied bed for my three hospitals last quarter, which Gemini converts into a report specification chosen from the closed catalogue of metrics and dimensions. The user reviews the specification before it runs, and the system never executes raw queries produced by AI. A short what this report shows summary is drafted from the computed numbers, and every figure in it is verified by code.
Governance is explicit. Managers may create Custom metrics with a formula builder restricted to permitted fields, previewed and labelled Custom, not certified. Group administrators can promote a custom metric within the group, and HQ certifies metrics for general use, with an audit trail for every change. Reports run on rollups or the analytics store (Workstream Q4) with row limits, asynchronous jobs for large reports, caching and cost guards, and tenant isolation is enforced centrally. Every report carries a data-quality banner showing coverage and evidence strength, and any aggregate about workers applies a minimum group size so that individuals cannot be identified.
Data model. Metric, MetricVersion, MetricBinding to pages and documents, ReportDefinition, ReportRun, Schedule, ShareLink, ReportTemplate and CustomMetric.
Screens and experience. The manager sees a Report Studio with a builder and a gallery of templates, plus a Metric browser. HQ sees a certification workflow and a view of which pages still use legacy calculations.
AI role. Converting a request into a validated report specification from a closed schema, and drafting narrative from verified numbers. It never produces a query or a number.
Integration. Feature 24: Configuration Time Machine and Safe Restore
Why it matters. The platform is full of configuration: thresholds, task cards, roster templates, notification rules, SOP bindings, planner rules, dosing and age limits, display layouts and permissions, set at global, group, facility and pit levels. When behaviour changes, such as why did pit 2 stop alerting or why did workers receive this prompt, the cause is often a configuration change made weeks ago by someone who has forgotten it. The audit log records that a change happened but does not make it easy to browse history, see a readable difference, work out what was in force on a given date, preview the impact of a change, or restore an earlier state safely. The threshold configurator in the master prompt versions one kind of configuration; this feature generalises the idea to all of it and connects it to the Pit Time Machine.
Who uses it and when. Managers and group administrators review their own configuration. HQ administrators and support staff investigate behaviour changes. Auditors check what was in force on a date.
How it works. Treat all configuration as versioned, typed documents in a Configuration Store with scopes (global, group, facility, pit, user) that resolve in layers. Every change creates an immutable version with author, timestamp, difference, effective date and a reason that is mandatory for sensitive categories, plus approvals where policy requires them. The Config Time Machine page lets the user choose a scope and a configuration type and see a timeline of changes. Selecting a version shows a readable difference in plain language, for example Moisture low alert changed from 35 percent to 30 percent by Priya on 12 September, reason: sensor reads high. An Effective configuration on date X resolver computes the merged result for a given pit and date, which the Pit Time Machine uses in its state card.
Changes are made safely. Before saving, and before any restore, an Impact Preview replays the last N days under the proposed configuration and shows how many alerts, tasks and notifications would change and which pits are affected, labelled with a blast radius. Changes can be scheduled, staged by facility or percentage and, where the blast radius is large, require two approvals. Safe Restore brings back an earlier version as a new version, never rewriting history, and supports restoring selected fields only. Guardrail Rollback can automatically revert a change and notify the owner if it causes an anomaly, such as an alert volume spike beyond a set percentage or notification delivery failures. A Change Calendar shows scheduled changes, and Freeze Windows block or tighten changes during audits or pilots. A Config Drift view shows which facilities have departed from group templates, and a Diff between two facilities or pits helps support staff. Configuration can be exported as signed JSON for backup or cloning to a new facility, with validation on import. A What changed this week summary, drafted by Gemini from the change records with links to each version, helps managers keep track. Access is role-based, sensitive categories such as permissions are restricted, and every change and restore is audited.
Data model. ConfigDocument, ConfigVersion (document, scope, version, difference, author, reason, effective-from, approvals), ConfigResolution cache, ChangeSchedule, FreezeWindow and RollbackPolicy.
Screens and experience. The manager sees a Badlav ka itihaas page with a timeline, differences and a restore button. HQ sees a global timeline, impact previews and rollback policies. Support sees side-by-side comparisons.
AI role. Summaries of changes only. It makes no restore decisions.
Integration. The Master Threshold Configurator (G3), group policy templates (Volume 1 Feature 19), the SOP Studio, the Pit Time Machine, the audit log (C4), notification rules, the Decision Inbox for approvals, and the HQ data grid, whose edits to configuration collections must route through this store.
Build notes and guardrails. Migrate existing configuration into the versioned store step by step. Keep resolution deterministic and fast with caching. Handle references between configuration documents carefully, because a version may point to others. Validate restores against the current schema, since old versions may predate schema changes. Never restore secrets from history. Test with the fleet simulator.
Measure and honest limits. Time to find the cause of a behaviour change, configuration-related incidents, rollback usage and approval turnaround. A restore cannot undo actions already taken, such as notifications already sent, and schema evolution makes very old versions harder to restore.
Priority. Strong for platform maturity. Five to six engineer-weeks.

The attention budget
There is one risk that every feature in this volume shares, and it deserves its own warning. Almost all of these features ask a worker or a manager to tap something. If each is added without discipline, a worker’s day becomes a series of prompts, forms and questions, and the product that was meant to help them becomes a burden. Adoption would fall and data quality with it. I therefore recommend a design rule that the agent must follow: an attention budget. Each role has a maximum number of interruptive prompts and a maximum number of extra taps per shift, set in configuration. Every feature must declare how many prompts and taps it adds and must compete for a place inside the budget. The fatigue governor from Volume 1 enforces the budget, the Day Close checklist auto-fills as much as possible, Sense Check prompts only when a human observation adds real information, and any feature that cannot show a measurable benefit for its attention cost is a candidate for removal. Treat the worker’s attention as the scarcest resource after the node’s energy.
