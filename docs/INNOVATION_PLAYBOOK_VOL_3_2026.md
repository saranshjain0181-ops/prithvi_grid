Prithvi-Grid Innovation Playbook, Volume 3: Connected Compost Operations and Role-Based Dashboards
Prepared for the Prithvi-Grid founding team · 24 October 2026 · Volume 3: twenty-four new features designed to work together as one system
Purpose and scope of this volume
The enterprise upgrade master prompt described the foundations of the platform. Volume 1 of this playbook added twenty-four features around the Smart Spear, the evidence it produces and the growth of the business. Volume 2 added twenty-four features for the craft of making compost and for the management pages that surround it. This third volume adds twenty-four more, and it does not repeat anything from those three documents. Wherever a new feature builds on an earlier one, it names it explicitly as the master prompt, Volume 1 or Volume 2, so your agent can reuse what already exists instead of building it twice.
This volume has two emphases that your latest brief asked for. The first is compatibility and teamwork. With seventy-two features across the documents, the biggest risk is no longer that something is missing; it is that features become islands, each with its own idea of a pit, a batch, a task or a number, each notifying people separately, each needing its own screen. The first part of this volume therefore proposes four backbone features whose only purpose is to make every other feature cooperate: a common event language, a workflow engine that chains features together, a graph of shared entities and lineage, and a registry with automated compatibility tests. The second emphasis is dashboards. A compost operation is seen from very different heights: the supreme head of Prithvi-Grid looks at a fleet of thousands of nodes and a growing business, the head of a client looks at outcomes, money and risk, the facility manager runs a live yard, the supervisor walks the pits, and agronomists, finance, field service and compliance staff each need their own view. The third part of this volume designs one dashboard framework and eight role-based dashboards that are all built from the same widgets, numbers and navigation, so that what the supreme head sees is always a faithful roll-up of what the manager sees.
Between the two, in Part B, are twelve new features for the craft and management of turning organic wet waste into compost and manure that Volumes 1 and 2 did not cover: pile geometry and aeration, microbial inoculants and additives, covers and biofilters, a receiving gate with feedback to the waste source, a pre-processing station for shredding and dewatering, pile mapping rounds, seasonal readiness, a training academy with task certification, contractor reconciliation, an operating cost ledger, labour standards and workforce sizing, and a resident engagement and compost-return programme for townships.
The people this volume serves
The system has a ladder of users, and each rung asks different questions. The Supreme Head, meaning the founders and executives of Prithvi-Grid, asks whether the business is healthy, which customers are at risk, whether the fleet is reliable, what each node earns and what needs a decision today. HQ department heads, for agronomy, fleet and field service, customer success and support, finance and commercial, compliance and legal, and platform and AI operations, each ask the same kind of question about their own domain at greater depth. The Client Head, who may be a hospital director, a hotel general manager, a township committee president or a group sustainability head, asks whether composting is working, what it costs, whether the organisation is exposed to risk, and which decisions are waiting. The Facility Manager asks what is happening in the yard right now, what is late, and what needs approval. The Supervisor asks where to walk next and who needs help. The Safai Mitra asks what to do now. Technicians, contractors, auditors, inspectors and residents each have a narrower view that must also be consistent with everything above.
The compatibility principles
Everything in this volume follows five principles. First, one event language: every meaningful thing that happens in any feature is published as a named, versioned domain event, and other features react to events instead of calling each other directly. Second, one set of shared entities: a pit, a batch, a container, a lot, a worker, a scale and a node mean the same thing everywhere, and relationships between them are stored once in a graph that supports lineage. Third, one definition of every number: every figure on every dashboard comes from the Metric Dictionary described in Volume 2, so a number never differs between the manager’s screen and the supreme head’s. Fourth, one way to show it: every dashboard is composed from the same widget library and every object is shown with the same Universal Entity Card, so navigation and drill-down feel identical at every level. Fifth, one attention budget: every prompt, tap and notification a feature adds to a person’s day is declared, summed and capped, as described at the end of Volume 2.
To make this enforceable, every feature must satisfy a compatibility contract. It publishes a manifest that lists its dependencies and feature flags. It declares the events it publishes and consumes with their schema versions. It declares the dashboard widgets it contributes, with their data contracts and the roles allowed to see them. It uses the shared services rather than creating its own copies: the Configuration Store, the Metric Dictionary, the correction and amendment mechanism, the material and container library, the site graph, the equipment and scale register, the evidence chain and the AI Gateway. It declares its attention cost. It has permissions and tenant isolation tested by the generated cross-tenant suite. It uses Gemini only through the gateway with a non-AI fallback. And it ships contract tests and a journey test that prove its hand-offs to neighbouring features still work. Part A describes the machinery that makes this contract real.
Ground rules carried over
All the ground rules from the master prompt apply to every feature here. Deterministic code makes decisions, and Gemini is the only AI provider, used for explanation, language, perception and drafting through the AI Gateway. Every sensitive action is audited and every evidence-bearing record is sealed. Tenant isolation is enforced centrally. Ground-level features work offline. Every user-visible string is internationalised in Hindi, Hinglish and English. Anything involving law, regulation, product claims, wages, safety or science is tagged LEGAL-REVIEW or EXPERT-REVIEW, and Hindi wording is tagged NEEDS-NATIVE-REVIEW. Estimates are labelled as estimates. Dashboards never present a number without a way to see how it was calculated and how much evidence stands behind it.
How each feature is written
Every feature uses the same headings so that any one of them can be pasted into a build prompt. Why it matters describes the situation in the yard or the business. Who uses it and when names the roles. How it works gives the logic in enough detail for an agent to design it. Data model lists the records to add. Screens and experience describes what each role sees. AI role states precisely what Gemini does, and often that it does nothing. Team play is new in this volume and is the heart of the compatibility requirement: it lists the events the feature publishes, the events it consumes, the dashboard widgets it contributes and to which roles, and the features it depends on. Build notes and guardrails lists the traps. Measure and honest limits lists the numbers to watch and what the feature cannot do. Priority labels the feature Must-win, Strong or Opportunistic with a rough effort estimate in engineer-weeks for a human-reviewed agent build.
Event names follow one convention, lowercase and dot-separated: domain, entity and verb, for example pit.load.completed, pit.turn.completed, batch.stage.changed or scale.check.failed. Using the same convention in every feature description is deliberate, so your agent can lift the names straight into the event catalogue.
Part A: The compatibility backbone
Feature 1: The Event Spine, a Domain Event Catalogue and Publish-Subscribe Backbone
Why it matters. Today a typical feature learns about the rest of the system by querying tables it should not own, or by being called directly from another feature’s code. That works for ten features and collapses at seventy. A turning session should be able to tell the whole system it happened, once, and every interested feature, the verification engine, the cost ledger, the Day Close, the supervisor board, the supreme head’s rollup, should react in its own way without the turning feature knowing they exist. The same mechanism makes dashboards trustworthy, because they are built from the same events that drive the operations.
Who uses it and when. Developers and the build agent use it constantly. HQ platform staff use the Event Explorer to diagnose problems. Support staff use it to answer what happened to this task. Nobody on the ground sees it, but everyone depends on it.
How it works. Every domain event has a standard envelope: a unique event ID, type and schema version, tenant and facility, references to the entities involved (pit, batch, container, worker, equipment and so on), the actor (person, device or system), the corrected occurrence time and the server receive time, a correlation ID that follows an end-to-end journey, a causation ID pointing to the event that caused this one, a payload validated against a registered schema, references to evidence (readings, photos, scans), a confidence value where relevant, a source (device, app, import, system), an audience tag saying which roles may see it, and a personal-data flag. Events are written using the outbox pattern: the business change and its event are committed together, and a relay then delivers the event to the queue, so an event is never lost and never emitted for a change that failed. Consumers are idempotent and keep a processed-event ledger, ordering is guaranteed per entity by partitioning on the entity ID, and every consumer can be replayed from a time window to rebuild its projections, which is how dashboards, the unified event index and the entity graph are rebuilt after a bug fix.
A schema registry holds every event type with its versions, and compatibility rules allow only additive changes within a version and require a new version for anything breaking, with a deprecation window. The first catalogue should be organised into families: device and telemetry events; pit events (load, water, turn, cover, geometry, additive, alert); task and verification events; material and stock events; gate, preparation and container events; batch and stage events; quality and sample events; product, order and dispatch events; people, training and attendance events; cost and finance events; compliance, risk and correction events; configuration events; and AI and platform events. An Event Explorer lets staff search by entity, correlation ID, type or time and trace a journey step by step. Dead-letter handling, retention tiers, redaction of personal data in older events and an audit link for sensitive events complete the picture. The existing unified event index from Volume 2 becomes simply one projection of the spine.
Data model. EventEnvelope, EventSchema and EventSchemaVersion, Outbox, ConsumerSubscription with its checkpoint, ProcessedEventLedger, DeadLetter and EventRetentionPolicy.
Screens and experience. HQ platform staff see the Event Explorer, a schema catalogue with consumers per event, a lag and dead-letter monitor and a replay tool with safeguards. Developers see generated documentation of every event and who consumes it. Support staff see a journey view for a correlation ID.
AI role. Gemini may summarise a long journey for a support engineer from the stored events, labelled as a summary with links to each event. It has no role in event delivery.
Team play. The spine publishes platform events such as event.consumer.lagging and event.deadletter.growing. It consumes nothing domain-specific, but every other feature in all three volumes publishes to it and subscribes through it. It contributes widgets to the Platform and AI Operations cockpit (consumer lag, dead letters, replay activity) and is a dependency of the Dashboard Composer, the Orchestrator and the Entity Graph.
Build notes and guardrails. Migrate existing direct calls gradually, beginning with the highest-traffic flows (telemetry, tasks, alerts). Never put personal data in a payload unless flagged. Keep payloads small and reference large items. Replays must be tenant-scoped and rate-limited, and must never re-send external notifications.
Measure and honest limits. Event delivery latency, consumer lag, dead-letter rate, replay frequency and the share of features that communicate only through events. An event-driven system is harder to reason about than direct calls, which is why the Event Explorer and journey view are part of the feature and not an afterthought.
Priority. Must-win, and the first thing to build. Six to eight engineer-weeks.
Feature 2: Kaam Chain, a Cross-Feature Workflow Orchestrator
Why it matters. Composting is a chain of dependent steps spread across many features: waste is collected, inspected at the gate, pre-processed, mixed and loaded, checked for heat after a day, watered or turned, sampled at maturity, graded, bagged, sold and reconciled. Each step lives in a different feature, and nobody owns the whole chain. When a step is late or skipped, the next one silently starts from a wrong assumption. A workflow engine that models these chains explicitly, with owners, timers and escalation, is what turns many good features into one operating system, and it gives the manager a view of where each batch actually is.
Who uses it and when. Managers watch chains on the Control Room. Supervisors receive chain tasks in their plan. Workers see only the next task. HQ agronomists and operations staff author and approve chain templates.
How it works. A Chain Definition is data, versioned in the Configuration Store. It lists steps, each with a trigger (usually a domain event such as pit.load.completed or timer elapsed), an owner role, a required certification, an expected duration and a service level, branching conditions (if the pit is acid, go to the rescue chain), compensation steps for cancellations (the pit was emptied unexpectedly), and the human task or automated action to perform. Automated actions are limited to a safe catalogue: create a task in Aaj ka Plan, send a notification through the governor, request a forced node reading, open a decision, create a sample, change a batch stage. Humans always perform physical work. The engine stores a Chain Instance for each running chain with the state of each step, durable timers that survive restarts, and idempotent step execution, so an event delivered twice never creates two tasks.
Starter templates should cover the Loading Chain (collect, gate, prepare, load, first heat check), the Turning Cycle, the Acid Crash Rescue Chain, the Batch-to-Bag Chain (cure, screen, sample, grade, release, bag), the Sample-to-Grade Chain, the New Worker Onboarding Chain, the Contractor Month-End Chain, the Seasonal Readiness Chain, the Node Fault Chain and the Resident Complaint Chain. A no-code builder lets authorised users adjust templates within guardrails: only approved step types, bounded timers and no steps that would bypass a safety or permission check. A dry-run mode replays history or simulator data through a proposed chain to show where it would have created tasks and delays. Each chain publishes its own cycle-time and delay metrics, which feed the dashboards, and a Chain Map visualises every instance and the step it is in, with the slowest step highlighted.
Data model. ChainDefinition and version, ChainInstance, StepRun, ChainTimer, ChainEscalation and ChainTemplate.
Screens and experience. The manager sees a Chain Map of active chains with delays, and a chain detail page with a timeline. The supervisor sees today’s chain tasks inside Aaj ka Plan. The worker sees one next task with no mention of the chain. HQ sees a template library and an authoring screen with dry-run.
AI role. Gemini explains in plain language why a chain is stuck, using stored state and events, and can draft a chain definition from a manager’s description for a human to review and validate against the step catalogue. It never starts or advances a chain.
Team play. The engine consumes events from every operational feature and publishes chain.instance.started, chain.step.completed, chain.step.overdue and chain.instance.completed. It depends on the Event Spine, the Configuration Store and Aaj ka Plan. It contributes widgets for chains in flight, delayed chains and cycle time by chain to the Facility Control Room, the Supervisor Field Board, the Agronomy and Quality Cockpit and the Supreme Head Command Deck.
Build notes and guardrails. Keep the step catalogue small and well tested. Prevent infinite loops with a step budget per instance, and keep human tasks inside the attention budget by letting the governor batch and delay chain-generated tasks. Every state change is audited.
Measure and honest limits. Chain cycle time, share of chains completed on time, number of stuck chains and manager-reported clarity. Chains impose structure, and a chain that does not match how a facility really works will be ignored, so templates must be configurable and adoption watched carefully.
Priority. Must-win. Six to eight engineer-weeks.
Feature 3: The Entity Graph, Lineage and the Universal Entity Card
Why it matters. A compost operation is a chain of transformations. Waste from a kitchen goes into a container, the container goes to a gate, the waste is prepared, mixed with materials, loaded into a pit, becomes a batch, moves to a cure pile, is screened, sampled, bagged into lots, sold to a buyer and counted in a report. Every question that matters to a client head or an inspector cuts across that chain: which bags contain the batch that crashed in March, which reports used the scale that drifted, which buyers received compost from last month’s contaminated load, which kitchen produced the waste that went into this pit. Today each feature knows only its own link in the chain. A shared graph of entities and relationships makes these questions answerable in seconds, and a single standard card for showing any entity keeps every page and dashboard consistent.
Who uses it and when. Managers and auditors use lineage during disputes, recalls and inspections. Client heads use the entity card whenever they click a number. Supervisors use it to jump from an alert to a pit to a batch. HQ support uses it to investigate a tenant’s problem. Every dashboard uses it.
How it works. Define an entity registry with a stable identifier scheme for every kind of entity in the system: waste source, container, load, pit, batch, cure pile, finished lot, product, order, buyer, sample, node, scale and other equipment, material lot, supplier, contractor, document and, with strict privacy controls, worker. Relationships are stored in a relationships collection with a type (contains, derived-from, consumed-in, produced-by, sampled-from, sold-to, located-in, performed-by, verified-by), the two entities, validity dates, and the event that created the edge. A dedicated graph database is not required at this scale; a well-indexed relationships collection with bounded traversal depth is the boring, proven choice and keeps operations simple. A projection built from the Event Spine creates and updates edges automatically as events arrive, for example pit.load.completed links the load to its source containers, materials and pit, and batch.stage.changed links a cure pile to its batch.
On top of the graph sit lineage services. Forward lineage answers which products, orders and buyers are affected by a batch. Backward lineage answers what went into a lot: sources, dates, materials, scale readings and people (the people dimension is shown as roles or aggregated counts unless the viewer has permission). Impact analysis answers what changes if a record is wrong, for example a scale found out of tolerance on specific dates, listing every mass entry, batch, report and certificate that relied on it so the manager can decide what to amend. The same service supports recalls and dispute resolution, and results can be exported as a sealed evidence bundle.
The Universal Entity Card is the standard component for showing any entity anywhere. It has a header with name and status, a few key metrics pulled from the Metric Dictionary, the most recent events from the spine, open items (tasks, decisions, corrections), related entities from the graph, permitted actions, the thread from Volume 2, an evidence-strength badge and a button to open the entity in the Pit Time Machine. Every entity has a deep link such as prithvi://entity/batch/ID so QR codes, WhatsApp messages and notifications open the same card. Global search spans entity types with filters and semantic search using Gemini embeddings. Traversal always respects the viewer’s tenant and role, and the graph is checked regularly for orphans and broken edges.
Data model. EntityRef registry (type, ID, tenant), Relationship (from, to, type, valid-from, valid-to, source event), LineageSnapshot for heavy batches, ImpactReport and SavedLineageQuery.
Screens and experience. A lineage view draws the chain left to right for any batch, lot or container, with filters for dates and a switch between forward and backward. An Impact Report page lists affected records with a recommended correction path. Entity cards appear as pop-overs and side panels everywhere, and on phones as bottom sheets.
AI role. Gemini turns a natural-language question such as which bags came from the pit that crashed in March into a structured lineage query chosen from a closed schema, which the server validates and runs, and then explains the result in plain language with links. It never writes the query that executes.
Team play. It consumes events from the gate, preparation, loading, batch, finishing, sampling, product and dispatch features and publishes lineage.updated and impact.report.generated. It contributes widgets such as Affected records for impact analysis to the Compliance and Risk Cockpit, a Source-to-Sale trace to the Client Head Cockpit and Recent related items to the Facility Control Room. It is a dependency of the Dashboard Composer.
Build notes and guardrails. Cap traversal depth and result size, precompute lineage for completed batches, and make graph rebuilds from the spine a routine, tested operation. Treat worker edges as sensitive and expose them only through permitted views. Entity IDs must never be reused.
Measure and honest limits. Time to answer a lineage question, share of entities with complete lineage and number of orphans. Lineage is only as complete as the events recorded, so the page shows gaps where an expected link is missing instead of guessing.
Priority. Must-win. Five to seven engineer-weeks.
Feature 4: The Feature Registry, Compatibility Matrix and Contract Tests
Why it matters. The compatibility contract from the start of this volume is only a good intention unless a machine checks it. Without enforcement, features drift: one renames an event, another adds three prompts that blow the attention budget, a third is switched on for a tenant without the feature it depends on, and a dashboard widget silently shows nothing. With seventy-two features and an AI agent writing much of the code, automated, cross-feature checking is the only way to keep the system coherent. It also gives HQ a clean way to package features into plans and switch them on safely per tenant.
Who uses it and when. The build agent and developers use it on every change. HQ product and operations staff use the Feature Control screen to enable features for tenants. The Supreme Head sees adoption and compatibility health.
How it works. Each feature has a machine-readable manifest that records its ID, version, owner, dependencies (other features, shared services, flags, minimum versions), the events it publishes and consumes with schema versions, the widgets it contributes with data contracts and role permissions, its configuration keys, its Metric Dictionary entries, its permissions and routes, the entities and relationships it creates, the Gemini features it uses by gateway ID, its internationalisation namespaces, its migrations, its attention cost in prompts and taps per role, and its expert-review tags. A registry service stores the manifests and builds a Compatibility Matrix, a view of which features interact through which events, widgets and configuration, and a dependency graph.
Continuous integration enforces the contract. Manifests must validate. Event schemas must remain backward compatible, and consumer-driven contract tests mean every consumer declares what it expects from an event, so a provider cannot change an event without the consumers’ tests passing. Widget data contracts are tested against the Metric Dictionary. Every route in the manifest must have a permission entry, and the cross-tenant test generator is built from the manifest. Internationalisation coverage must be complete. The summed attention cost of all enabled features by default must stay within each role’s budget. The AI-provider guard confirms Gemini is the only provider. Beyond unit-level checks, journey tests exercise whole chains with the fleet simulator, for example load, heat check, turn, sample, grade, bag, sell and reconcile, running nightly in staging so that a broken hand-off is found within a day.
Feature Control lets HQ enable a feature for a tenant only when its dependencies are enabled at compatible versions, previews what will change (new screens, new prompts, new notifications), warns when disabling something that others depend on, and defines packages such as Basic, Compost Pro, Enterprise and Township from feature sets. Usage analytics per feature report adoption, attention cost and the outcome metric each feature claims to move, so features that cost attention without delivering value can be retired.
Data model. FeatureManifest, FeatureInstance (tenant, status, version), CompatibilityRule, ContractTestResult, JourneyTest and its runs, and FeaturePackage.
Screens and experience. HQ staff see a Feature Control page with a dependency graph, a compatibility matrix and per-tenant status. Developers see failing contract tests with a clear explanation of which consumer breaks. The Supreme Head sees feature adoption and attention-cost summaries.
AI role. Gemini may draft changelog summaries and describe the compatibility impact of a pull request for a human reviewer. It has no authority over merges or flags.
Team play. It consumes feature.enabled and feature.disabled events and publishes registry.compatibility.failed and journey.test.failed to the platform channel. It contributes widgets for feature adoption, compatibility health and attention-budget usage to the Supreme Head Command Deck and the Platform and AI Operations cockpit. It depends on the Event Spine and the Configuration Store and underpins the Dashboard Composer by validating widget contracts.
Build notes and guardrails. Generate manifests from code annotations where possible so they do not rot, make failing the contract tests block merges, and keep journey tests fast enough to run nightly. Packages must never grant access to features that depend on a missing foundation.
Measure and honest limits. Number of cross-feature regressions caught before release, time to enable a feature safely and the share of features with passing journey tests. Contract tests catch structural breaks, not every behavioural one, so journey tests and monitoring remain essential.
Priority. Must-win. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Part B: Craft and management features for compost and manure making
Feature 5: Pile Geometry and Aeration Designer
Why it matters. Oxygen reaches the middle of a pile only so far. If the pile is too tall or too wide, the core turns anaerobic, methane builds and odor follows no matter how often it is turned. If it is too small, it cannot hold heat and never reaches the hot phase. In pits and bays the depth and width are fixed by construction, in windrows the shape is chosen daily, and most workers simply pile as much as fits. The structure and aeration of a pile, such as coarse base layers, perforated pipes or bamboo channels, or a timed blower, are the physical design that determines whether the biology works, yet the system treats the pile as an unstructured volume.
Who uses it and when. Workers measure and shape the pile at loading. Supervisors check the design. Managers and HQ agronomists review pit designs and plan new pits. Technicians and masons use the drawings when building.
How it works. Pile Design Profiles are expert-defined per pit type (in-ground pit, brick bay, windrow, drum or in-vessel unit), waste type and season. A profile gives recommended maximum height, width and length, the base layer thickness, aeration channel layout (spacing of perforated pipes or bamboo poles) and cover guidance, with ranges and with adjustments for material porosity from the material library. At loading, a worker measures with a marked stick or rope using a simple Dher ki unchai screen: tapping the mark reached for height, with pictorial guidance on measuring from the side and never stepping on the pile. For windrows the worker also marks width. The system estimates volume from standard shapes with an uncertainty range, feeds that to the overload engine and mass estimates, and compares the geometry to the design, warning gently when the pile is too tall or too narrow.
An Aeration Check task records whether channels are clear (a stick inserted into a pipe, a simple blocked-or-clear icon) and creates a cleaning task when they are blocked. Where the facility has a blower, the Aeration Scheduler uses a duty cycle chosen by temperature feedback rules (on for a number of minutes in each period, adjusted by temperature and methane within limits), with manual override, a log of run time and energy, and a fail-safe local timer if the cloud is unreachable. Blowers and relays are a hardware proposal and need a licensed electrician; the software is ready to send timer commands through the command queue to a controller. Over time the system compares geometry with anaerobic episodes and heating rate for the pit and feeds that to Pit Personality, so the recommended geometry becomes specific to the pit. A Design Assistant for new pits takes expected daily intake and retention time and recommends the number and size of pits and the geometry, and prints a cross-section drawing as an SVG or PDF for the mason. It is reused by Survey-to-Quote.
Data model. PileDesignProfile, GeometryMeasure (pit, time, height, width, length, method, volume estimate), AerationChannel (pit, type, position, status), AerationSchedule and AerationEvent.
Screens and experience. The worker sees a stick illustration with a green zone and a red zone and taps the mark reached. The manager sees a pit design page with current geometry against the design and a list of recommendations. HQ agronomists edit profiles with approvals.
AI role. Gemini may estimate pile height from a photo that includes a marked reference stick, shown as a suggestion for confirmation, and phrases advice in Hindi. All geometry calculations are deterministic.
Team play. Publishes pit.geometry.measured, aeration.channel.blocked and aeration.schedule.changed. Consumes pit.load.completed, telemetry methane and temperature episodes, config.version.activated and pit.personality.updated. Contributes widgets such as Piles outside design (manager, supervisor), Blocked aeration channels (supervisor) and Geometry versus anaerobic incidents (agronomy cockpit), and Aeration energy cost to the cost ledger. Depends on the material library, the site map and the command queue.
Build notes and guardrails. Profiles are EXPERT-REVIEW items. Measuring instructions must never encourage standing on a pile. Blower electrical work and any hardware controller are recommendations for qualified engineers, and the system must not control power outputs without a safe fallback.
Measure and honest limits. Share of piles within design, anaerobic episodes by height class, heating rate and aeration energy per kilogram. Shape approximations carry error, and a controller is optional hardware that many facilities will not have.
Priority. Strong. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Feature 6: Microbial Inoculant and Additive Programme
Why it matters. Facilities add many things to piles: cow dung slurry, effective microorganisms, jaggery and buttermilk preparations, commercial cultures, lime, wood ash, neem, biochar and rock dust. Practices vary by supervisor, preparation is often botched (a culture used before it is activated or after it has expired), doses are guessed, money is spent without evidence and some additives, such as too much lime, can do harm. Nobody knows what works at which site. A structured programme with an approved list, preparation tracking, dosing guidance and genuine trials turns folk practice into learning.
Who uses it and when. Workers prepare and apply additives. Supervisors schedule. Managers see spending. HQ agronomists maintain the library and design trials.
How it works. The Additive Library is an expert-approved list. Each additive has a purpose (inoculate, buffer pH, adsorb odor, add nutrients), form, preparation steps with timers, dose ranges by pile mass and situation, cautions and incompatibilities, storage and shelf life, supplier and cost. The Preparation Station tracks culture batches: for an activated culture the worker records start time, container, ingredients and expected ready date, receives reminders, performs simple readiness checks with icons (smell, appearance), and the batch has an expiry that blocks use after the shelf life. The Dosing Assistant takes the pit state (for example acid crash), the estimated mass and the approved library to propose a dose range and method, and records the application with quantity and links it to stock.
Trials turn practice into knowledge. An agronomist or manager defines a trial with a hypothesis, the pits compared (matched using Pit Personality so they are genuinely similar), the metrics decided in advance (time to hot phase, odor episodes, days to maturity), duration and stopping rules. Trials are barred where safety is involved. When complete, the system analyses results with confidence intervals and reports them as Indicative unless the sample is large enough, always listing confounders. Results update a programme library, but recommended additives change only through expert approval. Cost-effectiveness compares additive cost per tonne with the measured effect. With consent, anonymised trial results can be pooled across tenants so every site benefits from every trial.
Data model. Additive, CultureBatch, AdditiveApplication, Trial (design, arms, status, results) and ShelfLifeRule.
Screens and experience. The worker sees culture cards with step timers and a big Lagaya button. The manager sees additive stock and spend. HQ agronomists see a trial designer and a cross-site results board.
AI role. Gemini drafts plain-language trial summaries from computed statistics and extracts fields from supplier datasheets into draft library entries for expert confirmation. It makes no dosing decisions.
Team play. Publishes additive.applied, culture.batch.ready, culture.batch.expired and trial.completed. Consumes pit.alert.raised (for example acid crash), inventory.low, pit.personality.updated and batch.stage.changed. Contributes Cultures ready today (supervisor), Additive spend (manager, cost ledger), and Trial board (agronomy cockpit). Depends on the material library, inventory and Pit Personality.
Build notes and guardrails. Claims about additives are LEGAL-REVIEW and library content is EXPERT-REVIEW. Show handling and PPE notes for caustic materials such as lime. Pre-register trial metrics to avoid cherry-picking, and label small trials as Indicative.
Measure and honest limits. Time-to-heat difference, odor episodes, spend per tonne and trial completion. Small samples and confounders limit certainty, so the system speaks in ranges and never declares a winner too early.
Priority. Strong. Five to six engineer-weeks.
Feature 7: Cover and Biofilter Manager
Why it matters. A cover on a pile is the cheapest odor, fly and moisture control a facility has. A tarpaulin, a thin soil layer, a cap of dry leaves or biochar keeps flies out, holds in odor, stops rain from soaking the pile and the sun from drying it. In practice covers are forgotten after turning, left open overnight, torn and never replaced, or used inconsistently, and the odor complaint that follows is blamed on the compost process. For enclosed or ventilated units, a biofilter bed does the same job for exhaust air and needs its own moisture and media maintenance. The Weather Playbooks in Volume 1 tell workers to cover the pit before rain, but the system has no idea whether the pit is actually covered right now, for how long it has been open or what type of cover is on it.
Who uses it and when. Workers set and confirm cover state at the end of every task. Supervisors audit open pits during rounds. Managers review exposure and cover inventory. HQ agronomists analyse which cover types work.
How it works. Every pit has a cover state: Open, Covered with a type (tarpaulin, soil layer, dry-material cap, biochar cap, net, lid) or Partly covered. The state changes with one tap, with an optional photo, and the system records the time so it can compute exposure minutes. Rules define the maximum open time by weather and odor risk: much shorter before rain, in strong wind towards a sensitive zone from the site map, or when the odor risk is high. When the Palatna Coach, the loading flow or watering ends, the app asks the worker to confirm the cover, and the Day Close checklist reads the state of every pit. A pit that exceeds its limit raises a reminder to the worker, then to the supervisor, through the fatigue governor.
Smart cover rules link several features. If a rain forecast arrives, the system adds a cover task and checks that cover stock is sufficient. If wind is strong, it asks for the cover to be secured. If odor risk is high and wind blows toward a sensitive zone, it recommends covering and delaying turning until the wind shifts. Cover assets (tarpaulins, nets, lids) are tracked in the equipment register with condition and a replacement plan, and a torn cover can be reported with a photo. A Cover Audit uses Gemini on a supervisor’s photo to suggest whether the cover looks lifted, torn or wrongly placed, always for human confirmation.
The Biofilter module covers sites with enclosed or ventilated composting units. Each bed has media type, installation date, moisture checks by squeeze test, pressure drop recorded manually, outlet odor on the smell scale and a refresh schedule. It plans media replacement and alerts when checks show channelling or drying. Over time the system learns the effect of cover state and type on odor episodes and moisture swings for each pit and reports it to Pit Personality and the agronomy team.

Feature 8: Receiving Bay Gate and Source Feedback Loop
Why it matters. Contamination is the most common cause of poor compost and equipment damage. Plastic, cutlery, bones, bags, excess oil and stray non-organic items arrive in bins from the kitchen, banquet or ward, and they end up in the pit, in the screener and finally in the buyer’s garden. Segregation scores in the master prompt measure the problem after the fact. What is missing is a formal point at the door where waste is inspected, accepted, sorted or rejected, and a feedback loop that goes back to the department that produced it. Without that loop the same contamination arrives every day and the composting team is blamed for what the kitchen sends.
Who uses it and when. Workers at the receiving bay inspect every container or a defined sample. Supervisors resolve rejections. Managers and client heads see source scorecards. Kitchen, banquet and ward leads receive feedback.
How it works. At the bay a worker scans the container QR (from the Waste Age Clock) and completes a Gate Inspection with large icons: contamination level (none, minor, major), contaminant types (plastic, cutlery and metal, glass, bones and large items, excess oil or liquid, packaging, suspected biomedical), and a photo. The decision is Accept, Accept after sorting (recording sorting minutes and contaminant weight) or Reject (return to source or send to a disposal route with a rejection slip). A suspected biomedical item immediately stops acceptance, holds the container, and opens the incident path with a banner reminding staff that biomedical waste is outside the scope of the system and governed by separate rules. Contaminants removed are logged by category and weight with their disposal route.
The feedback loop is the distinctive part. After each inspection with a finding, the system sends the source department a short feedback card in its language, by WhatsApp or the in-app inbox, with the photo, what was found and what to do, worded positively and specifically. A weekly summary shows each source’s trend. A Source Scorecard (by department, never by individual) shows contamination rate, rejection rate and sorting time caused, and the estimated cost of contamination (sorting labour and any equipment damage) feeds the cost ledger. Repeated major findings generate a Decision Inbox item for the client head or manager to hold a short coaching session, which links to the Training Academy. Recognition for improving departments is available as a green badge. Gemini can suggest contaminant types from the photo for confirmation.
Data model. GateInspection, ContaminationFinding, SortingRecord, RejectionSlip, SourceFeedback and SourceScorecard.
Screens and experience. The worker sees three large outcome buttons after the inspection icons. The manager sees scorecards, a contamination trend and the cost of contamination. The client head sees department comparison. Source leads see their own feedback and trend on a simple page.

Feature 9: Pre-Processing Station for Shredding, Dewatering and De-packaging
Why it matters. The size of the pieces and the amount of free liquid decide how fast waste composts and how much leachate it produces. Whole coconut shells, corn cobs, large bones and packaged items slow decomposition and damage the screener. Liquid-laden waste such as soups, curries and drained fruit pulp floods the pit and starts it anaerobic. Chopping and shredding increases the surface area microbes can attack, and draining or pressing removes excess water. Most facilities load waste as it arrives. Where a shredder or dewatering rack exists, its use is unmanaged: nobody knows its throughput, how often it is down, whether pieces meet a size target, or what the preparation is worth.
Who uses it and when. Workers operate the station. Supervisors manage the queue. Managers review throughput and cost. HQ agronomists define preparation rules by material.
How it works. The Pre-Processing Station is modelled as a stage with sub-stations: sorting table, shredder or chopper, dewatering rack or press, de-packaging area and mixing bay. Preparation rules attached to materials in the library say what each needs: shred if pieces exceed a size, dewater if moisture is estimated above a limit, de-pack if packaged. Each incoming container, after the gate inspection, is routed accordingly, and a Prep Plan lists what to do. Workers log Prep Sessions: input weight, output weight, liquid drained, runtime, equipment and operator. The system computes throughput, loss and yield, tracks backlog (containers waiting and their age, linked to the Waste Age Clock) and forecasts how long the backlog will take to clear.
A quick Size Check uses a ruler or fist-size rule with icons (most pieces smaller than a fist, a few larger, many larger) and an optional photo, so the system knows whether the target was met. Drained liquid is routed to the Leachate Loop. Runtime updates the equipment register for maintenance, and energy or diesel use is logged for the cost ledger. Safety is built in: a lock-out and guard checklist before the shredder starts, PPE confirmation and a certification gate so that only workers certified in the Training Academy can run it. The system correlates size class with heating rate after loading, so managers see the benefit of good preparation in their own data, and a Kaam Chain step schedules shredding to complete before the loading window.
Data model. PrepStation, PrepPlanRule, PrepSession, SizeCheck and LiquidSplit.
Screens and experience. The worker sees prep cards with the container, the required step and large start and finish buttons, plus the safety checklist. The supervisor sees the backlog. The manager sees throughput, size compliance and cost per tonne. HQ edits preparation rules.
AI role. Photo-based size suggestions for confirmation and phrasing of instructions.

Consumes gate.inspection.completed, pit.load.planned, training.certified and material.library.updated. Contributes Prep backlog (supervisor board, control room), Size compliance and heating benefit (agronomy cockpit), Cost per tonne of preparation (cost ledger, finance views) and Energy used (client head). Rollout. Start by logging sessions and backlog, then add size checks and the safety gate.

Feature 10: Pile Mapping Rounds, a Hot-Spot and Cold-Spot Survey
Why it matters. The Smart Spear measures temperature at one place. Real piles have gradients: the core is hotter than the edges, the top is cooler than the middle, and pockets of dry or compacted material form cold spots. Decisions about turning, moisture and pathogen reduction assume that the node’s reading represents the pile. Volume 1’s Safe Compost Proof honestly states that a single probe limits coverage. Experienced composters know this and probe the pile at several points with a long-stem thermometer, but their observations are never recorded. A structured mapping round makes that practice systematic and turns the single-probe limitation into a measured offset.
Who uses it and when. Workers or supervisors perform a mapping round weekly per active pile and at defined moments. Managers see cold spots. Agronomists use representativeness data. Auditors see sealed surveys attached to certificates.
How it works. A Mapping Round template defines a grid for each pile type, for example three by three points across the top and two depths, shown on a schematic with numbered points. The worker inserts a calibrated long-stem probe at each numbered point from the side and enters the reading with the visual calculator or by voice, with optional squeeze-test icons for moisture. Every reading records position, depth, time and instrument. The system builds a heat map with explicit uncertainty, not a falsely smooth image, identifies hot and cold spots and computes an evenness index. It compares the Smart Spear reading with the survey average on the same day and stores a Representativeness Offset for the pit, for example the node reads two degrees above the pile average, with a confidence that grows as rounds accumulate.
That offset is used in three ways. Engines can correct or widen their thresholds accordingly. The Safe Compost Proof can state its coverage honestly. And technicians can use it to advise where to place the node on installation. Mapping is scheduled by event as well as by time: before a turning decision, after an anomaly, and mandatorily at the points a certificate requires. A cold edge suggests an improved turning pattern, such as moving outer material into the core, and the suggestion is added to the plan. The thermometer is a calibrated instrument in the register, and every survey is sealed by the evidence chain.
Data model. MappingRoundTemplate, MappingRound (pit, points with position, depth, temperature and moisture category, instrument, time, worker), RepresentativenessEstimate and HotColdSpot.
Screens and experience. The worker sees a schematic, taps a point and enters the number. The manager sees a heat map, the evenness index and a cold-spot fix suggestion. Agronomists see representativeness across pits. Auditors see the sealed survey in the certificate pack.
AI role. Phrasing of the interpretation only. The map and offsets are computed by code.
Team play. Publishes survey.completed, pit.representativeness.updated and pit.coldspot.detected. Consumes batch.stage.changed (ready for certificate), pit.turn.completed and equipment.calibration.updated. Contributes Cold spots to fix (manager, supervisor), Representativeness by pit (agronomy cockpit) and Certificate evidence coverage (compliance cockpit). Depends on the equipment register, Pit Personality and the evidence chain.
Build notes and guardrails. Probing is done from the side and never by standing on the pile. Label interpolated maps as estimates. Support offline entry and keep rounds short.
A day with it. On the day a batch is due for its certificate, the supervisor completes a twelve-point survey in eight minutes. The map shows an even pile and a node offset of two degrees, and the certificate carries a coverage statement a buyer can trust.
Rollout. Begin with the weekly round on a few pits, then add event-triggered rounds and the certificate link.
Measure and honest limits. Rounds completed, evenness index and stability of the node offset. Twelve points still sample a small part of a large pile, and manual probe readings carry instrument and handling error.
Priority. Strong, because it supports certificates and engine accuracy. Three to four engineer-weeks.
Feature 11: Seasonal Readiness Programme
Why it matters. A composting site in central India lives through three very different seasons. The summer is hot and dry, so piles lose moisture quickly, workers face heat stress and fire risk rises. The monsoon brings heavy rain, flooded drains, soaked piles, leachate overflow and flies. The cooler months slow microbial activity, so piles heat slowly and need insulation and patience. Each season needs preparation weeks ahead: drains cleared, covers purchased, shed roofs repaired, bulking material stocked, workers retrained, nodes checked for sealing, thresholds adjusted. The Weather Playbooks in Volume 1 react to the forecast for the next day or two, but nothing manages the slower work of getting a facility ready, and most sites discover on the first heavy rain that they were not.
Who uses it and when. Managers own the programme for their facility. Supervisors and workers carry out items. Client heads and group heads see readiness before each season. HQ agronomy and operations publish season templates and watch fleet-wide readiness.
How it works. A Season Programme template, written and reviewed by agronomists and operations staff, defines a set of readiness items for each season with lead times, for example start monsoon readiness six weeks before the usual onset. Items fall into categories. Infrastructure covers clearing drain channels, checking pit base slope, repairing shed roofs and securing covers. Materials covers raising the target days of cover for bulking material, buying tarpaulins and stocking cover soil. Equipment covers testing pumps and checking hoses. People covers refresher training on rain handling or heat safety through the Training Academy. Devices covers a pre-monsoon node care round to check seals and glands. Procedures covers seasonal SOP variants. Configuration covers scheduled changes of thresholds and planner rules to seasonal profiles through the Configuration Store, with a freeze window so changes are not made accidentally mid-season.
Each readiness item has an owner, a due date, the evidence that closes it (a photo, a checklist, a stock count), a status and dependencies, for example tarpaulins need a purchase decision that appears in the Decision Inbox with the cost from the budget. A transparent Readiness Score for each facility combines item completion weighted by importance, and is shown with the list of what is missing. The system does not rely only on the calendar for the season itself. It watches weather data against configurable rules (for example cumulative rainfall or a sustained temperature range) to detect that a season has actually started, and then creates tasks to switch to the seasonal mode and activates the seasonal configuration. After each season, a Season Review is generated automatically from the data: weather-related alerts, incidents, readiness score against outcomes, what was late, and recommendations that feed back into the template. A local event calendar (festivals, wedding seasons, conference periods) feeds expected waste surges into planning, and an Annual Operations Plan combines seasons, maintenance shutdown windows and budget phases. HQ can push season templates to all tenants as policy templates and see readiness across the fleet.

Screens and experience. The manager sees a Season Readiness page with a countdown, a score and a checklist with photo evidence. The supervisor sees seasonal items inside Aaj ka Plan. The client head sees a one-line readiness status per facility. HQ sees a fleet readiness map and the template editor.
AI role. Gemini drafts the Season Review narrative from computed facts and translates checklist wording. It makes no readiness judgments.
Team play. Publishes season.readiness.updated, season.mode.activated and season.review.completed. Consumes weather.forecast.updated, inventory.low, equipment.fault.reported, training.completed, nodecare.round.completed and config.version.activated. Starts a Seasonal Readiness Chain in the Orchestrator. Contributes Readiness score (manager, client head, Supreme Head), Weather-exposed pits (supervisor) and Season review (client head). Feature 12: Training Academy and Task Certification
Why it matters. Many composting tasks are risky or quality-critical: running a shredder, handling lime, sampling for a laboratory, treating pests, working near hot piles, entering a confined area, operating a loader. Today training is informal, one worker shows another, nobody records who is competent at what, and a new worker can be assigned any task on the first day. The Skill Passport in Volume 1 is a credential, but nothing creates the skills it certifies, and nothing stops an untrained person from being given a hazardous job. A light but real academy, with practical sign-offs and a gate that checks certification before assigning a task, protects workers, improves quality and gives clients something to show an auditor.
Who uses it and when. New and existing workers learn in short sessions. Supervisors perform practical sign-offs. Managers see skills coverage. HQ agronomists and safety advisers publish courses. Contractors see certification coverage for their staff.
How it works. A curriculum is organised in paths by role: new worker, shredder operator, sampler, pest-control assistant, supervisor. Each path has modules, which are two-to-four-minute lessons with pictures and Hindi audio, authored in the SOP Studio so the training content and the procedures are always the same material. Scenario questions test judgement with images and audio, such as the smell is sharp and the pit is wet, what do you do, and a worker can answer by tapping or by voice. Passing a module is not enough for hazardous tasks. A practical sign-off requires a supervisor to observe the worker performing the task and tick a competency checklist, with a photo or time stamp, and for the highest-risk tasks a second sign-off from a safety-qualified person.
A certification has a validity period and a refresher schedule, and an incident or near-miss can trigger targeted retraining. The Task Certification Gate is the enforcement: a task type can require one or more certifications, and Aaj ka Plan and the roster assign only certified workers. If an uncertified worker must be used, a supervisor can authorise supervised work with a reason, which is logged and visible. A mentor pairing suggests an experienced buddy for new workers. The Skills Matrix shows, for each competency, how many certified workers there are on each shift, and flags dangerous gaps such as only one certified shredder operator, so the manager can plan training or rostering. Training time is recorded as work time and its cost enters the cost ledger. Audio-first design and short modules accommodate low literacy. Gemini helps authors draft quiz questions and audio scripts, and can propose a score for a spoken answer, but the supervisor always confirms any grade.

AI role. Drafting questions and scripts, translating content and suggesting scores for spoken answers, always with human confirmation.

Feature 14: Operating Cost Ledger and Budget
Why it matters. Every client head eventually asks the same question: what does composting cost us per kilogram, per month and per property, and are we within budget? The ROI tracker in the master prompt covers benefits, but the cost side is scattered across the subscription, labour, materials, water, energy, equipment, contractors, laboratory tests and the disposal of rejects. Without a ledger, nobody can say what a crash really cost, whether a shredder pays for itself or whether contamination from the kitchen is expensive. A transparent cost ledger, with costs allocated to pits, batches and products by visible rules, turns operational data into the financial language that owners and finance teams use for decisions, and it gives the Supreme Head a defensible account of the value clients receive.
Who uses it and when. Facility managers review it weekly. Finance staff and client heads review it monthly and during budgeting. HQ finance and customer success use it in business reviews.
How it works. The ledger has cost categories: labour (internal and contractor), materials (bulking and additives), water, energy and fuel, equipment depreciation and maintenance, consumables and PPE, laboratory and testing, transport and disposal of rejects, platform subscription and optional overheads. Most entries are derived automatically from events the system already holds: labour hours from attendance and task durations multiplied by rate cards, materials from inventory movements and unit costs, water litres from the Water Budget multiplied by a tariff, equipment cost from runtime, contractor cost from approved reconciliation statements, and laboratory cost from sample records. Feature 15: Labour Standards and Workforce Sizing
Why it matters. Managers decide headcount by habit. Too few workers and tasks are skipped; too many and cost rises quietly. Contractors price by headcount, and negotiations rest on opinion. Yet the platform already records how long turning sessions, loads, watering rounds and screening sessions actually take, in which conditions. That history can be turned into engineered standards for each task and used to calculate how many people a facility needs, now and in the coming season, giving managers and contractors a fair, evidence-based planning tool. It must be used for planning and never as a way to push individuals to work faster.
Who uses it and when. Managers use it during roster and contract planning. Client heads use it in budgeting. Contractors use it to propose staffing. HQ uses it to improve templates and quotations.
How it works. The Standard Time Library holds, for each task type and context (pit size, method, walking distance from the site graph, conditions), robust statistics such as the median and the seventy-fifth percentile of logged durations after outliers and implausible entries are cleaned. Times are split into fixed parts (preparation, PPE, travel) and variable parts (per hundred kilograms or per cubic metre). To avoid ranking individuals, durations are stratified by equipment and experience group and published only as aggregates with a minimum sample size. New sites start from expert-reviewed baselines until their own data accumulates, with a visible confidence indicator.
The Workload Model forecasts the work for the coming days and weeks from SOP frequencies, the intake forecast and banquet events, the season programme, maintenance and training needs, and converts it into labour hours by day, skill and zone. The Capacity Plan counts the hours actually available after leave, certification limits, heat and ergonomic limits and legal rest rules (tagged LEGAL-REVIEW). The Sizing Recommendation gives the minimum headcount per shift to meet the workload with a configurable buffer and a utilisation target, identifies peaks such as banquet weekends or the post-monsoon clean-up, and suggests shift patterns, for example moving heavy work into cooler hours. A scenario tool answers what-if questions: what if intake rises thirty percent, what if we add a pit, what if we adopt a shredder. Real-time load balancing flags a shift that is falling behind its plan and suggests reassigning someone from a lighter zone, and the Day Close compares planned and actual minutes. Idle capacity is shown as a resource to be used for improvement work such as node care or training. The policy is explicit and displayed to workers and contractors: standards are planning tools and are not performance targets for individuals, and any use in pay or contract terms needs legal review.
Data model. StandardTime, TaskContext, WorkloadForecast, CapacityPlan and SizingRecommendation.
Screens and experience. The manager sees a sizing page with required versus planned hours by day, a peak calendar and a scenario panel. Contractors see the same evidence when negotiating. Workers see nothing about standards, only a lighter or heavier day in their plan.
AI role. Gemini explains a recommendation in plain language from the computed numbers and drafts the staffing rationale for a contract discussion. It computes nothing.
Team play. Publishes workload.forecast.updated, sizing.recommendation.created and shift.behind.plan. Consumes task.completed with durations, session events from the coach, loads and screening, roster.published, season.mode.activated, banquet events and equipment.fault.reported. Contributes Required versus planned labour (manager, client head, contractor), Shift behind plan (supervisor, control room) and Labour hours by task (cost ledger). Depends on the site graph, the roster and the Ergonomic Guard.
Roles, permissions and privacy. Standards are visible as aggregates. Individual durations are never displayed to managers as comparisons between named workers.
Failure modes and fallbacks. With little data the system shows baselines labelled as such, and it refuses to recommend headcount when the data is too thin.
A day with it. Before a wedding week the manager sees a forecast peak of forty percent more labour hours on Saturday, adds one worker for the morning shift and moves watering to the evening, and the plan holds with no skipped tasks.
Rollout. Start by publishing standards internally to HQ, then use them in sizing recommendations, and only later share them in contract discussions.
Measure and honest limits. Accuracy of the workload forecast, tasks skipped for lack of labour and cost per tonne. Logged durations include waiting and interruptions, so standards are rough guides and not engineering-grade time studies.
Priority. Strong. Five to six engineer-weeks.

Townships also have a uniquely powerful lever, which is that residents like compost for their gardens. Who uses it and when. Residents use a simple portal. The committee president and members see a summary. The township manager handles complaints and distributions. HQ sees participation across townships.

Feature 17: Dashboard Composer and Widget Library
Why it matters. If each dashboard is built as a separate page with its own queries and its own idea of a number, then the head of a client, the facility manager and the supreme head of Prithvi-Grid will eventually see three different totals, and the cost of maintaining dozens of bespoke pages will swamp the team. The answer is a single framework in which every dashboard is a composition of standard widgets that read certified metrics and show the same entity cards. Role-based dashboards then become configurations rather than separate products, any feature can contribute a widget without touching the others, and a roll-up from facility to group to HQ is guaranteed to be consistent.
Who uses it and when. Every user, every day. HQ product staff define default dashboards per role. Managers and group administrators customise within their permissions. The build agent contributes widgets with each feature.
How it works. A widget is defined by a contract. The contract lists an ID and title, the metrics it reads from the Metric Dictionary and its query specification, parameters (scope of tenant, group, facility, zone or pit; time range; comparison), the roles allowed to see it, a refresh policy, a drill target (an entity card, a page or a chain), and its states: loading, empty, error, stale and partial-data. Standard widget types are the KPI tile with a sparkline and an evidence-strength badge, the status grid (pits, nodes, facilities as tiles), the map with overlays, the timeline, the funnel or flow diagram for stages, the heat calendar, the sortable table, the task and decision list, the alert feed, the gauge, the comparison panel, a narrative block whose numbers are verified, and an embedded Universal Entity Card. Features register widgets through their manifests, and the Feature Registry validates every contract.
The Composer is a drag-and-drop editor on a responsive grid for phone, tablet and wall display. HQ defines a default dashboard for each role, and tenants can add, remove and rearrange widgets within what the role permits. Users can save personal views and share them. All dashboards support global controls: a scope selector (group, facility, zone), a time range, and cross-filtering, so clicking a facility or a pit filters every widget on the page. Drill-through is uniform: a tile opens its entity card or page with breadcrumbs, and every number has a How is this calculated? link to the Metric Dictionary. A Data Trust ribbon at the top of each dashboard summarises data freshness, coverage and evidence strength so a viewer can tell at a glance how far to trust what they see. Dashboards can be exported as a sealed PDF for a board meeting, delivered as a scheduled snapshot by email or WhatsApp (a Subah ka snapshot for managers), shown in wallboard mode (Volume 2 Feature 21), and annotated with comments. An Explain this widget button asks Gemini for a short narrative from the widget’s own data and definition, with numbers taken from the API and cited.
Performance is part of the design. Each dashboard has a query budget, widgets read from rollups and the analytics store, load progressively with skeletons, cache by scope, and show a stale indicator rather than blocking. Usage analytics show which widgets are opened and which are ignored, and unused widgets are candidates for removal in line with the attention budget.
Data model. DashboardDefinition, WidgetInstance, WidgetContract, DashboardShare, SnapshotSchedule and WidgetUsage.
Screens and experience. Users see their role’s default dashboard and a Customise mode. HQ sees a template library, a widget catalogue with the features that contribute each widget and a preview as any role.
AI role. The explain-this-widget narrative and drafting of a dashboard layout from a described need for a human to adjust. Gemini never changes a number.
Team play. Consumes widget registrations from the Feature Registry and data from the Metric Dictionary and the Event Spine projections. Publishes dashboard.viewed and dashboard.snapshot.sent for analytics. Contributes nothing itself but hosts everything: all features in all three volumes contribute widgets to it. Depends on the Metric Dictionary, the Entity Graph, the Registry and the Configuration Store.
Roles, permissions and privacy. A widget is hidden from a role that lacks permission for its data, and a shared dashboard re-evaluates permissions for each viewer. Worker-level data never appears on any dashboard except in aggregates above a minimum group size.
Failure modes and fallbacks. If a widget’s data source fails, only that widget shows an error state. If the analytics store is delayed, a Last updated badge appears. A dashboard never shows a blank screen.
A day with it. A new feature is released with one widget. Because its manifest declares the widget and the roles, it appears automatically on the manager’s default dashboard, and the supreme head’s roll-up includes its metric the following morning, with no custom screen work.
Rollout. Build the widget contract and six standard widget types first, migrate the existing manager home dashboard onto it, and then introduce the other role dashboards one at a time.
Measure and honest limits. Time to first meaningful paint, widgets per dashboard, share of numbers linked to the dictionary and widget usage. A composer invites clutter, so HQ should curate defaults and the attention budget should cap widgets per dashboard.
Priority. Must-win. Seven to nine engineer-weeks.

Feature 22: Agronomy and Quality Cockpit
Why it matters. The agronomists of Prithvi-Grid, and the quality leads of larger clients, are the guardians of whether compost is made well. They govern profiles, recipes and additives, judge whether alerts are real, decide which practices to spread and decide whether a batch is good enough to sell. Their tools today are charts for one pit at a time. With dozens of facilities and hundreds of pits they need a cross-fleet view that shows where the process is working, where it is failing and why, and a governance queue in which every change to the knowledge the system depends on is reviewed with its evidence.
Who uses it and when. HQ agronomists use it daily. Client quality leads and facility managers see the part that concerns their own tenant. The Supreme Head sees summaries on the Command Deck.
How it works. The Cockpit has five areas. Process Quality shows distributions across pits and batches of time to hot phase, time in band, anaerobic episodes, odor episodes and days to maturity, with filters by waste type, season, pit design and facility, and clusters of pits with similar personalities. Practice Effects relates outcomes to practices recorded in the other features: loading mix quality, waste age, geometry and aeration, additives and trials, cover state, preparation, watering and turning quality. It shows effects with intervals and caveats rather than asserting causes. Measurement Quality shows the agreement of human observations with sensors (Sense Check), laboratory results against sensor values, the representativeness offsets from mapping rounds, sensor trust distributions and calibration status, because science depends on trustworthy measurements. Product Quality shows grade distributions, certificate readiness, contamination at source and the drivers of quality. Model Performance shows the odor predictor’s precision, recall and lead time, the overload engine’s accuracy, virtual sensor error and the false-Fake rate of verification, with trends and the data volumes behind them.
A Governance Queue lists every proposed change to controlled knowledge: turning profiles, recipes, additives, SOP templates, material library values, grade profiles and thresholds. Each item shows its justification, the shadow-mode results and the number of pits that would be affected, and requires an expert approval that is recorded and sealed. An Expert-Review Register shows the status of all EXPERT-REVIEW and NEEDS-NATIVE-REVIEW tags. A Needs an Agronomist inbox collects unusual cases: conflicting signals, repeated recipe failures and pits that do not respond. A Research Workbench lets agronomists define a cohort of similar pits, compare them in the Pit Time Machine, and export de-identified data. Ustaad tips and trial results arrive for review here. Client quality leads see their own tenant’s data with tenant-level benchmarks only against anonymised aggregates that meet minimum group sizes.
Data model. ReviewQueueItem, ExpertReviewRegisterEntry, Cohort and ResearchExport, with metrics through the dictionary.
Screens and experience. A desktop-first workbench with linked charts, a cohort selector, a queue with diff and impact views, and one-click open in the Pit Time Machine. Client quality leads get a simpler tenant view on tablet.
AI role. Gemini drafts summaries of cohort findings and of review items with citations, and flags unusual patterns for human attention. It never approves a change.
Team play. Consumes outcomes and practice events from nearly all craft features and from the sampling, formulation, mapping and trial features. Publishes review.approved, review.rejected and cohort.exported. Links to the Pit Time Machine and the Entity Graph. Depends on the Composer, the Metric Dictionary, the Configuration Store and the evidence chain.
Roles, permissions and privacy. Cross-tenant views are anonymised and available only to HQ agronomy roles, and exports are de-identified and logged.
Failure modes and fallbacks. Thin data produces an explicit Too few observations message, not a misleading chart, and expired reviews are flagged.
A day with it. An agronomist sees that pits loaded with more than the recommended share of wet waste show twice the anaerobic episodes in July. She opens the governance queue, approves a revised house recipe after reviewing its shadow-mode results, and the change rolls out through the Configuration Store with an impact preview.
Rollout. Start with Process Quality and the Governance Queue, then add Practice Effects and Model Performance as data accumulates.
Measure and honest limits. Time to review proposed changes, number of validated practice improvements and measurement quality indicators. Observational data cannot prove causes, and the cockpit is deliberately careful in its language.
Priority. Strong. Six to seven engineer-weeks.
Feature 23: Compliance and Risk Cockpit
Why it matters. For hospitals and hotel chains, the most valuable thing the platform can say is not that composting works, but that the organisation can show it works, to an auditor, an inspector, an insurer or its own board. The evidence is spread across features: the compliance matrix, sealed documents, the coverage calendar, calibration status, SOP currency, training certifications, incident reports, notices and licences. Someone has to pull it together, notice where it is weak and decide what to fix first. On Prithvi-Grid’s side, the same pulling-together applies to the register of legal and expert reviews that must be cleared before launch and to the risks the platform carries across all its tenants. A single risk-oriented view turns scattered records into a clear picture of exposure and of readiness.
Who uses it and when. Client compliance officers and client heads use it weekly and before inspections. HQ legal and compliance staff use the cross-tenant view. The Supreme Head sees its summary on the Command Deck. Auditors and inspectors use a read-only variant through inspection mode.
How it works. The Cockpit has six areas. Evidence Readiness shows the compliance matrix by requirement with status and evidence strength, the Data Coverage Calendar gaps against the period and ranked, and the status of sealed documents. Obligations and Notices shows the compliance calendar of licences, certificates, vendor and laboratory accreditations and scale verifications, the notice tracker, the ledger of fines actually paid, and regulatory changes with the lines and clients they affect. Operational Controls shows SOP currency and acknowledgements, training certification gaps, equipment and scale calibration status, contractor document validity and configuration freeze windows. Incidents and Learning shows the incident and near-miss register, after-action items and their completion, and recurrence. Privacy and AI shows consent coverage, open data-subject requests, retention schedules, and the registers of sub-processors and AI features, including the tenant’s AI opt-out status. Inspection Readiness shows a score with its method, a mock-inspection checklist, and a one-click inspector mode.
Underneath is a Risk Register populated automatically from system conditions: a scale out of tolerance or overdue for verification, an expired or expiring licence, weak evidence on a claim that is being used in a report, open critical alerts, overdue corrections, an unresolved biomedical-suspected event, a contractor with an expired document or a configuration change made during a freeze window. Each risk has a source rule, an owner, a severity, a mitigation, a due date and a review status, and the register supports manual additions. A heat map shows likelihood against impact using configurable rules that are visible and versioned. The Audit Pack Builder lets the user select a period and scope and produce a sealed bundle with the matrix, evidence, coverage statement, SOPs in force, training records and incident summaries. HQ sees the same structure across tenants, with a view of which tenants carry the most risk, and a register of every LEGAL-REVIEW and EXPERT-REVIEW tag with owner and status, which is the machine-readable form of the launch gate. The cockpit never states that a client is legally compliant; it states which evidence exists, which is missing and what is overdue.
Data model. Risk, RiskRule, RiskReview, AuditPackDefinition and ReviewTagRegisterEntry.
Screens and experience. A risk-first layout, a list sorted by severity and due date with owners, a heat map, and the Audit Pack Builder. On a phone it shows the top five risks and upcoming deadlines.
AI role. Gemini summarises the risk picture and drafts mitigation checklists from the structured risk data and approved playbooks, and can answer questions about the register with citations. It does not judge compliance.
Team play. Consumes scale.check.failed, calibration.overdue, document.expiring, correction.late_amendment, incident.reported, notice.received, config.freeze.violated, training.expiring, sop.version.approved, contractor.document.expiring and review.tag.created. Publishes risk.raised, risk.closed and auditpack.generated. Links to the Entity Graph for impact analysis and to the evidence chain for sealing. Depends on the Composer, the Metric Dictionary, the document vault and the Orchestrator for mitigation chains.
Roles, permissions and privacy. Compliance officers see their tenant. HQ legal sees cross-tenant risk but not private business data beyond what is required. Inspector access is time-limited and logged.
Failure modes and fallbacks. If a risk rule produces noise, owners can snooze it with a reason and an expiry, and HQ can tune the rule. Missing data creates a Cannot assess status and does not default to Safe.
A day with it. The cockpit shows a scale verification due in eight days, a licence expiring in three weeks and weak evidence on one sustainability claim. The compliance officer assigns each to an owner, and the Audit Pack Builder produces the quarterly bundle with one click.
Rollout. Begin with evidence readiness, calendar and risk register, then add privacy and the HQ registers.
Measure and honest limits. Number of risks open and overdue, audit preparation time and issues found in inspections. The cockpit reflects the data in the system and not the whole legal landscape, so it supports, and does not replace, qualified human review.
Priority. Must-win for hospital and enterprise buyers. Six to seven engineer-weeks.
Feature 24: HQ Department Cockpits for Fleet, Support, Finance and Platform
Why it matters. The Supreme Head Command Deck is a summary. The people who run each department of Prithvi-Grid need depth in their own domain and a clean way to hand work to each other. The head of field service needs to know which nodes will fail and which visits to bundle. The head of customer success needs to know which tenants are slipping before they churn. Finance needs to know where money is stuck and what each node earns. The platform team needs to know whether ingestion, queues and the AI Gateway are healthy and what they cost. When these cockpits are built from the same widgets and metrics as everything else, a customer ticket can become a field visit and a payment failure can become a customer-success conversation without anyone retyping facts.
Who uses it and when. Each department head and their teams use their cockpit daily. The Supreme Head reaches them by drilling down from the Deck.
How it works. Four cockpits are composed from the framework. The Fleet and Field Service Cockpit shows the node lifecycle funnel, the distribution of node health scores, an energy-starvation forecast, firmware rollout status with canary results, command queue delivery rates, SIM data usage anomalies, calibration due dates, the truck-roll queue with a proposed visit plan that bundles nearby jobs, spares and returns, Node Care adoption and visits avoided, and installation pipeline progress. The Support and Success Cockpit shows tickets by service level, a tenant 360 view combining health score, feature adoption, evidence strength and open risks, onboarding progress against the thirty-sixty-ninety-day playbook, pilot scorecards, satisfaction and net promoter results, a churn-risk list with reasons, and the schedule of quarterly reviews. The Finance and Commercial Cockpit shows the revenue movement waterfall, invoices, collections and dunning stage, GST invoice status, unit economics by node, cohort and city, hardware capital use, pipeline and forecast and partner commissions. The Platform and AI Operations Cockpit shows ingestion lag, queue depth, dead-letter backlog, service-level objective burn, deployment health, security events, and the AI Gateway’s latency, error rate, cache hit rate, fallback rate, validation-failure rate and cost by feature and by tenant against budget.
Department handoffs are explicit chains in the Orchestrator rather than emails. A support ticket marked as a device problem starts a Node Fault Chain that creates a visit in the Fleet cockpit, notifies the client when a technician is scheduled and closes the ticket on verified recovery. A payment failure starts a dunning chain that alerts the success manager before any suspension. A firmware rollout that pauses automatically creates a decision for the fleet head and a note for support. An AI cost anomaly creates a task for the platform team and, if it affects a tenant’s cap, a message to the success manager. Each cockpit has action buttons that start these chains with the relevant entity already attached, and the Universal Entity Card appears everywhere so any department can open the same tenant, node or ticket.
Data model. The four dashboard definitions, plus handoff chain templates in the Orchestrator and the metric definitions for each department.
Screens and experience. Desktop-first dashboards with department-specific default layouts, saved views and the same drill-through and trust ribbon as other dashboards. Field service also gets a mobile technician view.
AI role. Draft summaries, suggested replies to tickets for human sending, and explanations of cost anomalies with citations. None of it acts alone.
Team play. Consumes device, telemetry, node-care, support, billing, onboarding, pilot, AI-gateway and platform events. Publishes handoff.created and handoff.completed. Contributes widgets to the Supreme Head Command Deck. Depends on the Composer, the Orchestrator, the Entity Graph and the Metric Dictionary.
Roles, permissions and privacy. Each cockpit is limited to its department role, with tenant data shown according to contract and need. Finance data is restricted, and support access to client data uses the audited view-as-client rules.
Failure modes and fallbacks. If a chain cannot start because a dependency is disabled, the action button explains why and offers the manual alternative.
A day with it. A hospital reports a dark camera image. Support taps Start Node Fault Chain, which asks the site for a lens clean through Node Care, verifies recovery by a forced image, and closes the ticket. No visit is needed, and finance sees the avoided cost.
Rollout. Build Fleet and Platform first because they carry the most operational risk, then Support, then Finance.
Measure and honest limits. Time from ticket to resolution, field visits per hundred nodes, collection time, and platform incidents caught before clients notice. Cockpits depend on clean entity data, and handoff chains must be tuned with the people who use them.
Priority. Strong. Eight to ten engineer-weeks across the four cockpits.
How everything works together: one day from kitchen bin to board room
The best test of compatibility is to follow one batch of waste through the whole system and see which features take part. At six in the morning the Aaj ka Plan, built by the planner from the Seasonal Readiness status, the weather, the stock levels and the open chains, appears on the supervisor’s Field Board and the manager’s Control Room. A banquet bin that was filled the previous night is listed by the Waste Age Clock and scanned at the Receiving Gate. The gate inspection finds plastic cups and publishes gate.inspection.completed. The Source Feedback Loop sends the banquet department a photo card, the cost ledger adds a small contamination cost, and the Entity Graph links the container to its source.
The Pre-Processing Station shreds the coarse material, with a certified operator confirmed by the Training Academy gate, and publishes prep.session.completed, which updates the equipment register and the cost ledger. The Parat Planner proposes a layer plan, the Pile Geometry design confirms the pile will stay within its limits, and the loading is logged as pit.load.completed. The Orchestrator’s Loading Chain notes the event, schedules a heat check for the next day, and the Wake Scheduler arranges for the node to be awake. The Cover Manager prompts the worker to cover the pit, and the Day Close later reads the state of every cover.
Over the following days the Water Assistant, the Palatna Coach and Sanjivani operate on the same pit. A Sense Check records a squeeze test that disagrees slightly with the moisture sensor and the calibration suggestion goes to a technician. A Pile Mapping round shows an even pile, so the node offset is small. When the batch matures, the Sample-to-Grade Chain starts, a laboratory result arrives, the grade is assigned and the batch moves through the Curing Line, the Screener and the Bagging step, where the scale check status determines whether the weights count as measured. The Mass Balance reconciles the numbers, the lot is released, the Offtake Planner assigns it to a buyer, or the Compost-Return Programme assigns it to residents, and the Cost Ledger closes the batch with its cost per kilogram.
Throughout, the Supervisor Field Board has shown the supervisor only what needed attention, the Control Room has shown the manager the stage flow and exceptions, and at day close the manager signed the Day Close. The Client Head Cockpit shows diverted and produced mass with evidence strength, cost per kilogram and the decision waiting about cover stock. The Compliance and Risk Cockpit shows the scale verification due and the audit pack ready. The Agronomy Cockpit shows how this batch compares with similar batches, and the Supreme Head Command Deck shows a rolled-up fleet number that is the sum of exactly the same certified metrics. Every step used the same event language, the same entities, the same definitions and the same attention budget, which is what compatibility means.

Appendix D: Attention budget ledger
The attention budget is only useful if it is concrete. The defaults below are starting points to be tuned with real workers and managers in your pilots. Each feature must declare its cost in the manifest, the Registry sums the costs for the features enabled in a tenant, and continuous integration fails if the default configuration exceeds a role’s budget.
Suggested default budgets per shift or day. For a Safai Mitra: at most eight interruptive prompts per shift (a prompt is anything that asks for an answer or an action that is not already on the plan), at most twenty-five extra taps beyond the core task flow, and at most five push or voice notifications. For a supervisor: at most fifteen interruptive prompts per shift and forty extra taps. For a facility manager: at most six interruptive notifications per day, with everything else delivered in two digests. For a client head: one weekly brief and critical items only. For HQ department heads: one daily digest and critical items. Critical safety alerts bypass every budget and are never counted against it.
Declared costs of the features in this volume. These are estimates that each feature should replace with measured values after pilots.
• Pile Geometry and Aeration Designer: two taps per loading to record height, and one reminder per blocked channel. No daily prompt.
• Inoculant and Additive Programme: one to two taps per application and one reminder when a culture is ready. No recurring prompt.
• Cover and Biofilter Manager: one tap at the end of each task, and at most two reminders per day per worker when a pit is overdue for covering.
• Receiving Gate: six to eight taps per inspected container, with sample-based inspection when volume is high. Source departments receive at most one feedback card per day.
• Pre-Processing Station: five taps per session and a safety checklist of four ticks per shredder start.
• Pile Mapping Rounds: up to twelve numeric entries once a week per active pile, planned as a task and not as an interruption.
• Seasonal Readiness: items arrive inside the normal plan with at most two extra tasks per week for a supervisor.
• Training Academy: lessons are scheduled paid-time tasks and do not count as interruptions, but a lesson is capped at four minutes and three per week.
• Contractor Reconciliation: no worker cost. Managers have one monthly task and exception resolutions as they arise.
• Operating Cost Ledger: no worker cost. Managers have a monthly review and optional invoice uploads.
• Labour Standards and Workforce Sizing: no worker cost. Managers have one weekly planning view.
• Resident Programme: no staff cost beyond complaint handling. Residents receive at most two campaign messages per month.
• Day Close: one task per shift for the lead, under five minutes, mostly auto-filled.
• Dashboards: no prompts. Scheduled snapshots are limited to one per day per user and they respect the digest rules.
Rules the governor enforces. Prompts are queued and batched by priority. A low-priority prompt that would exceed the budget is deferred to the next window or folded into a digest. Two prompts about the same pit within thirty minutes are merged into one. A prompt that has been dismissed three times in a row is retired for that user and reported to the feature owner as a design problem. Any feature that adds cost without a measured benefit after a pilot is flagged for removal in the quarterly review.

Appendix F: Access, privacy and retention summary
This summary gives the agent a consistent baseline for permissions. The exact matrix should be defined as data in the permission system, and every item concerning personal data, health, wages or retention is tagged LEGAL-REVIEW.
Roles. The Supreme Head and HQ executives see cross-tenant aggregates and client identities for contracted tenants. HQ department roles (agronomy, fleet, support, finance, compliance, platform) see only what their department needs, and support access to tenant data uses audited view-as-client rules. The Client Head sees aggregates for their organisation and its properties. The Facility Manager and Supervisor see operational data for their facility or zone, including individual task status for assignment and support. Workers see their own tasks, training and attendance. Contractors see only their own workers, rosters and statements. Auditors and inspectors have a time-limited read-only role scoped to chosen facilities and periods. Residents see only their own household or block information.
Data classes. Operational data (pits, batches, tasks, readings) is tenant-confidential. Labour data (names, phone numbers, attendance, training, certifications, symptom reports, location check-ins) is personal and, for health-related items, sensitive: it is stored separately, visible on a need-to-know basis, never used for automatic penalties or pay changes, and shown on dashboards only as aggregates above a minimum group size. Resident data is stored separately from labour data. Wage and rate data is restricted to finance roles. Photos and voice notes are personal data when they contain people or voices and are subject to short retention.
Consent and rights. Optional features that use personal data (household-level tracking, voice notes kept for training, Skill Passport sharing, health symptom reports) require explicit consent in a language the person understands, can be withdrawn at any time and take effect immediately. Workers and residents can see, correct and request deletion of their data, with the exception that sealed audit and compliance evidence is pseudonymised rather than deleted where the law requires retention.
Retention defaults to be confirmed. Raw telemetry and event detail follow the tiers in the master prompt. Photos used for supervision default to ninety days unless attached to a sealed record. Voice notes are deleted after transcription unless the worker opts in. Chat threads follow tenant policy with a legal-hold option. Training and certification records are kept for the validity period plus a configurable tail. Aggregated and de-identified data may be kept longer for research.
AI and personal data. Before any text, image or audio is sent to Gemini through the gateway, names and phone numbers are masked, images are checked for people where feasible, and the feature’s data classification determines whether it is allowed at all. Tenants can opt out of AI features, and every AI feature has a non-AI fallback.
